A local SEO audit is a check of how well your business shows up when people search for something near them. It looks at your Google Business Profile, your website, your listings, your reviews and your links, and it ends with a list of fixes in order of importance.

This guide gives you a 12-step local SEO audit you can do yourself, with a checklist, a worked example, a report template and the tools you need. It works for shops, clinics, restaurants, trades, agencies and any business that serves customers in a set area.

What is a local SEO audit?

A local SEO audit is a structured review of the things that decide whether your business appears in Google's local results, Google Maps and AI answers for searches such as "plumber near me" or "accountant in Leeds."

It answers four questions:

  • Can searchers find you, and is the information about you correct?
  • Do you look trustworthy compared with nearby rivals?
  • Does your website match what local searchers want?
  • What should you fix first?

Why should you run a local SEO audit?

  • Wrong details lose customers. A wrong phone number or old opening hours sends people to a rival.
  • Local results change. New rivals, new reviews and new Google features change who appears.
  • It shows quick wins. Most audits find simple fixes that take hours, not weeks.
  • It guides your budget. You see which fix is worth your time first.
  • It sets a baseline. You can measure progress against it.

Run a full audit once a year and a short check every quarter.

How does Google decide who appears in local results?

Google says local results are based mainly on three things: relevance, distance and prominence. Knowing them helps you read your audit.

Relevance is how well your profile matches what the person searched for. A complete profile with the right categories and clear services helps Google see that you fit the search. Distance is how far your business is from the searcher, or from the place named in the search. You cannot change where you are, but you can make sure your address and service area are right. Prominence is how well known your business is. It comes from reviews, links, mentions and how often people search for you by name. Google also says that good SEO practice helps your website's role in local ranking.

This is why the audit covers so many areas. The profile feeds relevance. The address feeds distance. Reviews, links and citations feed prominence. A gap in one of them can hold back the others.

Service-area businesses and more than one location

Some businesses go to the customer, such as plumbers, cleaners and mobile mechanics. These are called service-area businesses. They can hide their street address on their profile and list the areas they serve instead. The audit for them has two extra checks. First, confirm that the service areas match the places where you really work and do not list more than you can cover. Second, make sure your website has a page for each main area, with real detail about that area, such as typical jobs, local landmarks and customer comments.

If you have several locations, audit each one. Every site should have its own profile, its own page and its own reviews. Keep one row per location in your spreadsheet with its profile status, review count, rating, local page and monthly calls. This makes it easy to see which site needs the most work, and it stops one good location from hiding a weak one.

How to read your reviews like an auditor

When you read reviews for the audit, do not stop at the star rating. Look at the words customers use. If several reviews say "fast reply" or "clean and tidy," those are things you can put on your website and use as proof. If several mention long waits or unclear prices, those are problems to fix in the business, and no amount of SEO will cover them up.

Also look at how rivals reply. A calm, short reply that names the problem and offers to fix it shows future customers how you treat people. Copy that approach. Avoid long arguments, and never share private details about a customer in a public reply. Aim for a steady flow of new reviews rather than a burst, since a burst can look odd and a steady flow shows an active business.

How to write a local title and description

The page title is one of the clearest signals of what a page is about, and it is the first thing a searcher sees. For local pages, put the service first, then the place, then your name. Keep it near 60 characters so it is not cut off in the results.

For example, a weak title is "Home | Smith and Sons." A better one is "Emergency Plumber in Leeds | Smith and Sons." The second tells both people and search engines what the page is for and where. The meta description is the short text under the title. Use it to say what you offer, where and why to call, in a sentence or two. A good one reads like a helpful answer, not a list of keywords.

What local schema markup looks like

Schema markup is code that you add to a page to describe it to search engines. For a local business, it states the business type, name, address, phone number, opening hours, the areas you serve and links to your profiles. It does not change what visitors see. It helps search engines read the page correctly. Many SEO plugins for WordPress and other platforms can add it for you. In the audit, check that the details in the markup match your profile and your page exactly, since a mismatch sends mixed signals.

How voice and mobile searches change the audit

Many local searches are made on a phone, often while walking or driving, and some are spoken to an assistant. People use full questions such as "Where can I find a plumber open now?" Your audit should test for this. Check that your hours are correct and easy to find, that your phone number is clickable, that your pages answer common questions in plain words and that your address is easy to copy into a map. Short answers near the top of a page and a short FAQ section help here, since they match how people ask.

Before you start: what you need

  • Your exact business name, address and phone number (NAP) as you want it shown
  • Access to your Google Business Profile, website and Google Search Console
  • A spreadsheet for notes, with one tab for each area
  • A list of five to ten searches a customer would use, such as "[service] in [town]"
  • Your three closest local rivals

Use a private browser window when you search, so your history changes the results less. Also check from a phone, because local searches are often made on one.

Step 1: Check your Google Business Profile

This is the most important step for most local businesses. Open your profile and check every field.

Fix what is wrong as you go. Check also that no duplicate profile exists for the same business, since duplicates split reviews and confuse customers.

Step 2: Check your NAP and citations

A citation is any online mention of your business name, address and phone number, such as a directory listing. Search engines compare these to confirm your details.

  1. Search your business name and phone number in Google and note every listing that appears.
  2. Check the main ones: Google, Bing Places, Apple Maps, Facebook, Yelp where it applies, and the top directories for your trade or country.
  3. Write the details you find in a table and mark any that differ.
  4. Fix wrong details, and remove or merge duplicates.

Small differences count. "Street" and "St." or a different suite number can look like a different business. Use one format and repeat it exactly.

Step 3: Audit your reviews

Reviews affect both ranking and whether a customer picks you.

  • Count and rating. Compare your number of reviews and your average with the three rivals.
  • Recent activity. A page of old reviews looks stale. Look for reviews in the last three months.
  • Replies. Check whether you reply to reviews, good and bad, in a calm and short way.
  • Themes. Read the reviews for patterns: what customers praise and what they complain about.
  • Your process. Do you ask every customer for a review? A direct link by text or email makes it easy.

Do not buy reviews or write your own. Review platforms can remove them, and some countries treat fake reviews as unfair practice.

Step 4: Check your local keywords

Local keywords combine a service with a place, such as "emergency plumber Leeds" or "family lawyer near me." Do this:

  1. List your main services and the places you serve.
  2. Use a keyword tool to check volume for each combination.
  3. Search the top ones in Google and note who appears in the map results and in the normal results.
  4. Note which searches your business already appears for in Search Console.

Match each important keyword to one page on your site. If you have no page for a service or place that matters, add it to your fix list.

Step 5: Audit your website for local SEO

Open your site on a phone and on a computer. Check these things.

Pages

  • A page for each main service
  • A page for each location, with its own address, hours, map and details
  • A contact page with your NAP, a map and a form

Titles and headings

  • Each page title names the service and the place, and is under about 60 characters
  • Each page has one main heading and clear subheadings

Content

  • Real local detail: areas you serve, local examples, parking, landmarks and local reviews
  • No copied pages with only the town name swapped

Speed and mobile

  • Pages load fast on a phone
  • Click-to-call number and clear buttons

Schema markup

  • Local business data with your name, address, phone, hours and area served

Step 6: Check your map and local pack position

The local pack is the box of three businesses with a map at the top of many local results. Check where you appear.

  • Search each key local phrase from a phone, in a private window.
  • Note whether you appear in the pack, in Maps or only in the normal results.
  • Note who appears instead and what is different about them: reviews, distance, categories, photos or website.

Results change with the searcher's location. A business a few streets closer to the searcher may rank higher. That is normal. Look at the pattern across several searches, not one.

Step 7: Check your local links

Links from relevant local sites help you rank. Check who links to you and who links to your rivals.

  • Look for local news, chambers of commerce, trade bodies, schools, charities and local blogs
  • Note links your rivals have that you lack
  • Note any links from poor or unrelated sites

Plan honest ways to earn links: sponsor a local event, join your trade body, offer an expert tip to a local paper or partner with a nearby business. Do not buy links. Google's spam policies treat buying links to affect rankings as link spam.

Step 9: Check your tracking

If you cannot measure results, you cannot tell what works.

  • Is Google Search Console connected and verified?
  • Is Google Analytics set up with events for calls, form submissions and clicks on directions?
  • Do you use a call tracking number or ask customers how they found you?
  • Do you review the Business Profile performance report, which shows calls, direction requests and website clicks?

Step 10: Check technical basics

  • Indexing. Your key pages should appear in Google. Search for your page title in quotes to check.
  • HTTPS. The whole site should load securely.
  • Broken links and pages. Fix or redirect them.
  • Duplicate content. Merge near-copies.
  • Accessibility. Readable text, good contrast, labelled forms.

Step 11: Check AI search visibility

More people ask AI tools for local options. Test it yourself.

  1. Ask ChatGPT, Google's AI answers and another AI tool the questions a customer would ask, such as "best family dentist in [town]."
  2. Note whether your business appears, what is said about it and whether the details are correct.
  3. Note the sources the tools use, such as review sites or directories.

If details are wrong, fix them at the source: your profile, your site and your listings. There is no way to force an AI tool to name you, but accurate facts, good reviews and clear pages help.

Step 12: Write your fix list

Put every issue in a table with three columns: the problem, the impact and the effort. Then sort.

PriorityTypeExamples
1High impact, low effortWrong phone number, missing category, no review request process
2High impact, higher effortNew service pages, location pages, review growth plan
3Lower impact, low effortAdd photos, answer questions on your profile, fix small listing errors
4Longer termLink building, content program, reporting setup

Work from the top. Re-check after 30 days.

A worked example: a plumbing company

This example is illustrative. A plumbing company with one office covers three towns. It runs the 12 steps and finds this.

ProfilePrimary category is "Contractor"Change to "Plumber" and add "Water heater installer" as another
CitationsTwo directories show an old phone numberCorrect both and merge one duplicate
ReviewsFewer reviews than two rivals, no repliesSend a review link after each job and reply to all reviews
WebsiteOne page for all services, no town pagesCreate separate pages for the three main services and one for each town

The owner fixes the first three rows in one week. The new service and town pages take a month. After 90 days, the company repeats the audit and compares calls and direction requests with the first month.

What goes in a local SEO audit report?

If you share the audit with a boss, a client or a team, keep the report short and clear.

  1. Summary. Three or four sentences: the main findings and the top actions.
  2. Scorecard. A simple rating for each area, for example good, needs work, urgent.
  3. Findings. One short section per area with screenshots.
  4. Competitor comparison. The table from step 8.
  5. Action plan. The prioritized fix list, with owners and dates.
  6. Next review date. Usually in 30 to 90 days.

How long does a local SEO audit take?

A small business with one location can finish a first pass in a day. Several locations, or a large site, can take a week or more. The fixes take longer than the audit. Google says changes can take hours to months to show, so give them time and check progress in 30, 60 and 90 days.

Common local SEO audit mistakes

  • Looking only at the website. For local searches, the Business Profile often matters more.
  • Checking from one place. Results vary by location and device.
  • Ignoring reviews. They shape both ranking and choice.
  • Fixing nothing. An audit is only useful if you act on it.
  • Fixing everything at once. Work in priority order and measure.
  • Skipping the follow-up. Repeat the checks every quarter.

A quick local SEO audit checklist

  • Profile complete, correct category, hours and services
  • NAP identical across listings, no duplicates
  • Steady reviews and replies to all of them
  • A page for each main service and each location
  • Titles with service and place, local schema, fast mobile pages
  • A few honest local links
  • Calls, directions and form fills tracked
  • Checked in Google, Maps and AI tools
  • A prioritized fix list and a date for the next audit

Final thoughts

A local SEO audit shows where customers lose you and where they can find you. Do the 12 steps, fix the high-impact items first and check again in a month. Repeat the audit each year, and keep a short quarterly check in your calendar.
