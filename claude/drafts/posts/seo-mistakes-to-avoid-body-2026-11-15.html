The most common SEO mistakes are not clever tricks gone wrong. They are basic problems that stop a page from being found, understood or chosen: a stray noindex tag, content that loads only in the browser, duplicate or missing titles, thin pages that repeat what others say, weak internal links and slow pages. Check access and indexing first, then relevance and content, then speed and polish.

This checklist covers 12 mistakes in the order we would fix them. For each, you get how to spot it, how to fix it and the data behind it. We cite only sources we could read at the publisher, and we say when a figure comes from a vendor's own audits. It replaces our older post on the same topic.

What data shows how common SEO mistakes are?

Two public data sets help. They measure different things, so do not mix them.

  • The 2025 Web Almanac (HTTP Archive). It analyzes millions of web pages. It found title tags on 98.6% of desktop pages, but meta descriptions on only 67.7%, H1 headings on 71%, and canonical tags on 68%. It found a noindex directive on 3.5% of desktop pages and 2.4% of mobile pages, and structured data on 50% of pages. These are page-level facts for the crawled sample, mostly home pages, and a noindex on some pages is often intended.
  • SE Ranking's audit data. SE Ranking, an SEO software vendor, published the share of audited websites with each issue. It reported that 74.43% had images without descriptions, 53.69% had duplicate titles, 65.38% had missing meta descriptions, 54.67% had missing H1 headings and 50.58% had pages blocked by noindex. These are sites that ran an audit with its tool, which may skew toward sites with problems, so read them as a guide to what audits find, not as the share of all sites.

The pattern is that basic elements are widespread, and still often missing or wrong. That is why a plain checklist pays.

Mistake 1: Optimizing for a keyword, not for the search task

How to spot it: the page gets impressions but few clicks, people leave quickly, or the top results are a different kind of page. The problem: a page can use the right phrase and still answer the wrong question. Someone searching for a checklist wants steps. Someone searching for a service wants scope, proof and price.

The fix: before you write or update a page, classify the task: learn, compare, diagnose, do or buy. Look at the kinds of pages that rank for the query, and match the format and depth. Then answer the main question first and the natural follow-up questions after it. Our guide to ranking in AI Overviews shows the same answer-first approach.

Mistake 2: A noindex tag or a robots block you did not mean

How to spot it: a page is missing from Google even though it is live. Open Search Console's Page Indexing report and look for "Excluded by noindex tag" and "Blocked by robots.txt". In WordPress, check Settings, Reading, "Discourage search engines" and the robots setting in your SEO plugin.

The fix: remove the tag or rule on pages that should be found, then request indexing. This is the most costly mistake, because nothing else helps while it is there. A noindex on 3.5% of desktop pages in the Web Almanac sample is often right, for example on thank-you pages, so check page by page. Our robots.txt guide explains what the file can and cannot do.

Mistake 3: Content that search engines cannot see

How to spot it: use the URL Inspection tool in Search Console and compare the rendered HTML with what you see in the browser. Or fetch the page without a browser and look for the main text in the response.

The fix: make the main text, links and headings available in the HTML that is served, or reliably rendered. Google can process JavaScript, but other crawlers may not. In December 2024, Vercel reported that none of the major AI crawlers it tested rendered JavaScript. Avoid "load more" buttons for content you want indexed, and use normal links.

Mistake 4: Duplicate pages and mixed canonical signals

How to spot it: several URLs show the same content, such as with and without "www", with tracking parameters or in print versions. In Search Console, look for "Duplicate without user-selected canonical".

The fix: pick one preferred URL for each page. Link to it, list it in your sitemap, and point canonical tags to it. The Web Almanac describes canonical tags as strong hints, not commands, and found them on 68% of desktop pages in 2025. Do not send mixed signals, such as a page that is in your sitemap, canonicalized to another URL and blocked from crawling at once. Use redirects for old duplicate URLs.

Mistake 5: Weak titles and meta descriptions

How to spot it: run a crawl and sort by title. Look for repeated titles, missing ones and titles that are too long or empty of meaning. The Web Almanac found titles on almost every page, so the issue is quality, not presence. SE Ranking's audits found duplicate titles on 53.69% of sites, and missing meta descriptions on 65.38%.

The fix: write a distinct, accurate title for each page that names its subject, and a description that states what the reader gets. Google may rewrite either one if it does not match the page, so keep both honest. Avoid stuffing keyword lists.

Mistake 6: A messy heading structure

How to spot it: check each page for one clear H1, then H2s for the main sections and H3s for parts of them. The Web Almanac found an H1 on 71% of desktop pages, and only 66% had a non-empty H1, so many pages lack a clear main heading. SE Ranking's audits found missing H1s on 54.67% of sites and multiple H1s on 54.52%.

The fix: use one H1 that names the page's subject, then a logical outline. Use headings as a table of contents, not as places to repeat the keyword. A clear outline helps readers scan and helps systems pull the right passage for an answer.

Mistake 7: Thin or copycat content

How to spot it: the page says what the top results say, in fewer words, with nothing new. It has few links and no examples, data or experience.

The fix: add something others lack: first-hand experience, a worked example, a template, original data, an expert quote or a clear decision rule. Google's guidance for AI features says unique, expert-led content with a point of view will likely matter more over time than other tips. Make fewer, stronger pages instead of many thin ones. Google's spam policy on scaled content abuse lists using generative AI tools to produce many pages without adding value as an example.

Mistake 8: Keyword stuffing and pages that compete with each other

How to spot it: the same phrase appears in every heading and alt text, or two pages of your own alternate in results for the same query.

The fix: use the keyword where it fits and write for the reader. For pages that compete, merge them into the strongest page, redirect the weaker ones and update links. Pick one page for each main intent.

Mistake 9: Weak internal links and orphan pages

How to spot it: crawl your site and list pages with no or one internal link, and links whose text says "read more" or "click here". SE Ranking's audits found internal linking issues on a large share of sites, including 69.32% with pages that have no inbound links.

The fix: link new pages from related older pages as soon as you publish, use clear, short anchor text and link to the final URL, not a redirect. Keep important pages within a few clicks of the home page. Our guide to entity SEO shows how clear links and names also help machines understand your topics.

Mistake 10: Slow, unstable pages

How to spot it: check field data in Search Console's Core Web Vitals report and in PageSpeed Insights. Google's web.dev gives these recommended thresholds, measured at the 75th percentile of page loads: Largest Contentful Paint within 2.5 seconds, Interaction to Next Paint of 200 milliseconds and Cumulative Layout Shift of 0.1.

The fix: find the biggest cause for each template. Common ones are oversized images, render-blocking scripts, many third-party tags and images without set dimensions that make the layout jump. Speed does not replace relevance, but a very slow page can block the task.

Mistake 11: Images without useful descriptions, and heavy files

How to spot it: list images with missing alt text and files over a few hundred kilobytes. SE Ranking's audits found images without descriptions on 74.43% of sites.

The fix: write alt text that says what the image shows, in plain words, for people who cannot see it. Leave it empty for purely decorative images. Compress files and set width and height. Do not fill alt text with keywords.

Mistake 12: Markup that does not match the page, and no measuring

How to spot it: run key pages through Google's Rich Results Test. Check that the markup matches what the page shows. The Web Almanac found structured data on 50% of pages, mostly as JSON-LD in the HTML.

The fix: mark up only what is visible and true, such as Article, Organization and Product, and keep it current. Google says structured data is not required for its AI features, though it helps with rich results. Then measure. Use Search Console for impressions, clicks, indexing and, for AI features, the Generative AI performance report. Skipping measurement lets old problems return unseen. Why does Google show a different title or description than mine?

Google builds title links and snippets from the page and may replace what you supplied when it does not describe the page well. The Web Almanac notes that title rewrites in search results are widespread, yet title tags are still present on almost every page. So write titles that match the visible page, give the main heading, title and opening answer the same purpose, and put a clear definition or answer near the top of the page. If Google keeps changing your title, compare it with your H1 and with how other sites link to the page. The rewrite is often a sign that your title promises something the page does not deliver.

A bonus mistake: blocking the bots that feed AI answers

Security plugins and CDN settings sometimes block crawlers without anyone noticing. OpenAI says sites that opt out of OAI-SearchBot will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers, and that a robots.txt change can take about 24 hours to apply. Check that the search bots you want, such as Googlebot, Bingbot and OAI-SearchBot, are not blocked on pages you want found. Training bots such as GPTBot are a separate choice. What are the biggest SEO mistakes?

If you only fix a few things, fix these first, because each one can keep a page out of search results or hide it from buyers:

  1. A noindex tag or robots block on pages you want found.
  2. Content that search engines cannot see, such as text that only loads after a script runs.
  3. Pages that do not match what the searcher wants.
  4. Important pages that return an error, or redirect to the wrong place.
  5. Pages with no internal links pointing to them.

Do 404 errors hurt SEO?

A 404 means "page not found." Google says it does not use content from URLs that return a 4xx status, does not index them, and removes already indexed 4xx URLs from its index. It also crawls them less often over time. So a 404 is not a penalty. It is the right answer for a page that is gone on purpose.

The harm comes from the pages it removes. If a page that earned links or traffic starts returning a 404, you lose what that page earned. Here is how to handle it:

  • Page moved: use a 301 redirect to the closest matching page.
  • Page gone for good, with no close match: leave a 404 or 410 and make sure no internal link points to it.
  • Page should exist: restore it and fix the cause.
  • Error shown, but the page looks normal: check for a "soft 404," where the page says "not found" but returns a 200 status.

To find them, open the Page indexing report in Google Search Console, or crawl the site with a tool. On a large site, fix the error pages that have links or traffic first.

Common on-page SEO mistakes to avoid

On-page mistakes live on the page itself. They are usually the fastest to fix. The most common ones are:

  • A title or meta description that is missing, duplicated or does not match the page (mistake 5).
  • Headings that skip levels or carry no meaning (mistake 6).
  • Thin or copycat copy that adds nothing new (mistake 7).
  • Keyword stuffing, or several pages aimed at the same search (mistake 8).
  • No internal links to or from the page (mistake 9).
  • Images with no description, or files that are too large (mistake 11).

Common technical SEO mistakes to avoid

Technical mistakes stop pages from being found, shown or used. Check these first, because they can undo good content:

  • A noindex tag or robots block you did not mean (mistake 2).
  • Content that loads only in the browser, so crawlers cannot see it (mistake 3).
  • Duplicate pages and mixed canonical signals (mistake 4).
  • Slow, unstable pages on phones (mistake 10).
  • Markup that does not match what the page shows, and no measuring (mistake 12).

Mistakes to avoid when hiring an SEO company

Many SEO problems start with the wrong hire. Google's own advice for hiring an SEO is a useful checklist:

  • Never accept a ranking guarantee. Google says no one can guarantee a number one ranking, and warns against anyone who claims a special relationship with Google.
  • Ask for examples and a plan. Ask for past work, the results they expect, the timeframe and how they measure success.
  • Give read access first. For an audit, Google advises read access to Search Console and not write access at the start.
  • Insist on explanations. Be careful if a company is secretive or will not say what it plans to do. You are responsible for what firms you hire do to your site.
  • Do not trust cold emails. Be wary of firms that email you out of the blue.
  • Never agree to link to them or to buy link schemes.

How should you order the fixes?

PriorityMistakesWhy first
1. Access and indexing2, 3, 4If a page cannot be crawled or indexed, nothing else matters.
2. Relevance and content1, 7, 8The page must answer the right question better than others.
3. Structure and signals5, 6, 9Clear titles, headings and links help people and systems understand the page.

A free 60-minute audit

  1. 10 minutes: open Search Console's Page Indexing report and fix noindex and robots problems on pages that matter.
  2. 10 minutes: inspect three key pages with URL Inspection and compare the rendered text with the live page.
  3. 15 minutes: crawl the site with any crawler, and sort by title, H1, status code and inbound links.
  4. 10 minutes: read the Core Web Vitals report and pick the worst template.
  5. 10 minutes: review your top five pages against the search task and write what is missing.
  6. 5 minutes: list the first three fixes, with an owner and a date.

Worked example: a small services site

This is an illustration, not a client result. Imagine a 40-page site for a local accounting firm. The audit finds a noindex tag left on its services page after a redesign, a blog that loads posts only after a "load more" click, 12 pages with the same title, and a home page with three H1s. The team fixes access first by removing the noindex tag and adding normal links to blog posts. It then rewrites 12 titles and merges two competing posts. In week four it adds internal links from the blog to the services page, and compresses the largest images. It tracks indexed pages, impressions and inquiries against a baseline taken before the work. The result may be small or large, and it is measured, not assumed.

Final thoughts

Fix what stops a page from being found first, then what makes it worth choosing, then what makes it pleasant and fast. Keep a short audit habit, since a stray tag or a redesign can undo months of work. Measure the result in Search Console and in leads, not only in rankings.
