To rank in Google AI Overviews, your page must be indexed and eligible to show in Google Search with a snippet. Beyond that, Google says there are no extra technical requirements and no special tricks. What helps is the same work that helps any page: answer the question clearly, cover the related questions, back your claims with sources, and be talked about by other sites. Ranking well in normal results also helps, but it is no longer enough on its own.

This guide gives you a checklist of ten steps, with the data behind each one and the limits of that data. It also covers how to check whether Google cites you, what does not matter, two worked examples and a 30-day plan. We cite only studies we could read at the source, and we say when a finding is a correlation and not a cause.

What are Google AI Overviews, and when do they appear?

An AI Overview is a short AI-written summary at the top of some Google results, with links to the pages it drew on. Google says AI Overviews and AI Mode may use "query fan-out": the tool splits your question into several related searches and gathers supporting pages for each one. Google also says an AI Overview only shows when its systems decide it adds something to classic Search, so many searches do not trigger one.

Studies agree that they appear far more often on questions and long searches. Two examples, with different samples:

  • Ahrefs analyzed 146 million search results and found that AI Overviews showed on 21% of keywords. They showed on 57.9% of question searches and on 46.4% of searches with seven or more words. "Why" questions had the highest rate, at 59.8%. Of the keywords that triggered one, 99.9% were informational.
  • The Pew Research Center tracked the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025. About 18% of the Google searches in its sample produced an AI summary. That share was 8% for searches of one or two words and 53% for searches of ten words or more.

The two figures differ because the samples and methods differ. Both point the same way: AI Overviews are mostly about informational and question-style searches. If your pages target buying searches, such as "pricing" or "near me", expect fewer of them to be affected.

Pew also found that people clicked a regular result in 8% of visits to pages with an AI summary and 15% of visits to pages without one, and clicked a link inside the summary in 1% of visits. Google says clicks from pages with AI Overviews are higher quality. Look at conversions and not only clicks.

What does Google say you need to do?

Google's documentation on AI features is short and plain:

  • To be shown as a supporting link, a page must be indexed and eligible to show in Google Search with a snippet. There are no additional technical requirements.
  • The usual SEO basics still apply: allow crawling, link pages together, give people a good page experience, keep important content in text, and keep structured data matched to the visible text.
  • You do not need new machine-readable files, AI text files, special markup or special schema.org types.
  • Preview controls such as nosnippet, data-nosnippet, max-snippet and noindex apply to AI formats. More restrictive settings limit how your content can be used.
  • Google's guide asks for unique, non-commodity content with a point of view and first-hand experience. It warns that making separate pages for every variation of a question, mainly to influence AI answers, breaks its scaled content abuse policy.

Google also reports AI feature traffic in the normal Search Console performance data and in a Generative AI performance report. Use those for your own measurement.

Do you need to rank on page one first?

It helps, but the link is weaker than it used to be. In July 2025, Ahrefs found that about 76% of pages cited in AI Overviews also ranked in the top 10 for the same query, based on 1.9 million citations. In March 2026 it repeated the study with 863,000 keyword results and about 4 million AI Overview URLs and found about 38%. Ahrefs suggests that query fan-out explains part of the drop, because the tool draws on the results for related sub-questions. That is a guess, since Google does not publish the sub-questions.

The lesson is to rank for the question and for its follow-ups. A page that answers a related sub-question can be cited even when it does not rank first for the main phrase.

How to rank in AI Overviews: a 10-step checklist

1. Check which of your searches trigger an AI Overview

List 20 to 30 searches you want to be cited for. Run each in a logged-out window set to your country. Note which ones show an AI Overview. Work on those first. Skip the rest for now.

2. Make sure the page is eligible

Check that the page is indexed, returns a normal 200 status, is not blocked by robots.txt or your CDN, and is not set to nosnippet or noindex. In Search Console, confirm that the page is indexed. Our guide to robots.txt for SEO covers the access side.

3. Answer the question early

Put a direct answer in the first sentences under each question heading, in plain words. Then add detail. A reader and a tool can both use that answer without the rest of the page.

4. Write headings as real questions

People search with questions, and AI Overviews trigger most often on them. Use the wording your buyers use. Take the questions from sales calls, support emails and the "People also ask" box.

5. Cover the related questions, on the right pages

Ahrefs reports a study it ran with SurferSEO. Pages that ranked for the related fan-out searches were 161% more likely to be cited than pages that ranked only for the main phrase. The correlation between ranking for fan-out searches and being cited was 0.77. Treat that as a pointer, not a law. The practical step is to cover a topic in depth: write the main page, then pages for the real follow-up questions, and link them together. Write each page because a reader needs it. Do not create a page for every wording of a question.

6. Add evidence and what only you know

Name your sources, add dates, and show first-hand experience: your own data, tests and results. Google's guide asks for content that goes beyond common knowledge. A page that restates what everyone already says gives a tool no reason to choose it.

7. Build real mentions across the web

Ahrefs found that branded web mentions had a correlation of 0.664 with AI Overview visibility in its study of 75,000 brands, and that mentions on highly linked pages had a correlation of 0.70 across 76.7 million AI Overviews. These are correlations, so they do not prove cause. Google adds that seeking fake mentions is not as helpful as it might seem. Earn mentions with real stories, real data and real expertise.

8. Use more than text where it fits

Google says its AI features can bring in images and video, and that following its image and video guidance already counts. Ahrefs found that YouTube made up 18.2% of the AI Overview citations that did not rank in the top 100 for the same keyword, and 5.6% of all cited URLs in its 2026 study. If a video answers the question better than text, publish one, and write a clear title and description.

9. Keep pages fresh and markup honest

Update facts and examples on your best pages and show an honest update date. If you use structured data, make sure it matches what the page shows. Google does not require special markup for AI Overviews, so do not expect markup alone to change your result.

10. Measure with repeated checks

Check your list monthly: is an AI Overview shown, are you cited, and who is cited instead? Record the date and market. Use the Search Console reports for the Google side. Because AI answers vary from run to run, look at the trend over several checks and not at one result.

What does not matter?

  • Page length. Ahrefs found a near-zero correlation (about 0.04) between word count and AI citations. Google says there is no ideal page length. Write until the question is answered.
  • llms.txt and "chunking". Google says it does not use llms.txt files and that you do not need to break content into tiny pieces for AI.
  • Special schema. Google says no special schema.org markup is needed. FAQ markup also lost its rich result in Google Search on 7 May 2026.
  • Rewriting only for AI. Google says AI systems understand synonyms and general meaning, so you do not need to write in a special way.

Worked example 1: a software company's "what is" page

This is an illustration, not a client result. Imagine a company that sells expense software. Its page "what is expense management" ranks on page one, but an AI Overview shows for that search and does not cite it.

The team reads the cited pages. They are short, answer in the first two sentences, name a source for a statistic and include a one-line definition. The company's page opens with a long story about the company's history. The fix is simple. It moves a two-sentence definition to the top, adds a named source for the one statistic it quotes, and adds a short list of the steps in a typical process. It also writes a page on a fan-out question that the cited pages cover, "how do expense approvals work", and links the two.

The team does not rewrite everything. It sets a monthly check of 25 searches, records whether it is cited, and watches the trend over three months.

Worked example 2: a consultancy that is cited for definitions but not comparisons

Again, an illustration. Imagine a small consultancy that sells compliance training. It is cited in AI Overviews for three "what is" searches but not for any "X vs Y" or "best way to" searches, where buyers compare options.

The team finds that the comparison searches cite review sites and a trade association page, and that the consultancy appears on neither. The plan has two parts. It writes one honest comparison guide with a table, named sources and a note on when each option fits. It also approaches the trade association with a short, original guide based on its own audits. Success is measured by whether the cited pages for those searches change over the next three checks.

The lesson is to look at which sources are cited for each kind of search. Definitions, comparisons and how-to searches often cite different pages.

How do you optimize for AI Overviews?

AI Overviews SEO is mostly ordinary SEO done well. The checklist above gives the steps. In short: make sure the page is indexed and can show a snippet, answer the question clearly under a matching heading, keep facts accurate and current, show who wrote the page and why they know the topic, and check whether Google cites you for your key questions. Content for AI Overviews works best when one page answers one question in plain words.

What does it mean to rank in an AI Overview?

There is no rank number inside an AI Overview the way there is in a list of results. A page is either shown as a supporting link or it is not. Some sites are linked next to a sentence, some in a side panel and some at the bottom. So "ranking in AI Overviews" really means being cited, and sometimes being named in the text. Track whether you are cited, how often, and for which searches, not a position.

How does Google choose its sources?

Google says its AI features rest on its core Search ranking and quality systems. It uses a method it calls retrieval-augmented generation: the system finds relevant, current pages in the Search index, reads the passages and writes an answer that links to the pages that support it. Google does not publish a list of ranking factors for AI Overviews, and no third-party tool can see inside its systems. What the studies add is a set of patterns, such as the share of cited pages that also rank, which we covered above. Use them as hints, and test on your own searches.

Does this work for product pages or only for informational content?

Mostly for informational content. Ahrefs found that 99.9% of the keywords that triggered an AI Overview in its study were informational, and that question searches triggered them far more than other searches. Product and pricing searches show them less often. That does not mean product pages are out of scope. Google asks you to keep Merchant Center and Business Profile information current, and to support text with good images and video. But for most businesses, the larger gains come from guides, comparisons and answers to buyer questions that sit around the product.

How long does it take to see changes?

We found no reliable public figure, so we do not quote one. Pages must be recrawled before a change can show, and the answers themselves vary from run to run. Google also says that indexing and serving are not guaranteed, even when a page meets every requirement. A practical rhythm is to make a change, wait for the page to be recrawled, then check the same searches monthly for at least three months before you decide that it worked.

How do you check whether AI Overviews cite you?

  1. List your searches. Put the ones where you already rank on page one first.
  2. Run each in a logged-out window with your target country. Write down the date.
  3. For each, record whether an AI Overview appears, whether you are cited, which page, and who is cited instead.
  4. Copy every cited URL. The list shows who Google treats as a source in your market.
  5. Repeat monthly and compare. Keep AI Mode results separate from AI Overviews.
  6. Add Search Console's Generative AI performance report for Google's own numbers.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Chasing a trick. Google says there is no special optimization. Fix the basics first.
  • Writing a page for every wording of a question. Google warns that this can break its spam policy.
  • Burying the answer. A long introduction before the point gives a tool no clear passage.
  • Quoting unsourced statistics. A claim with no source is easy to skip and easy to dispute.
  • Ignoring the sources Google already cites. If you are on none of them, your own pages are only part of the answer.
  • Judging by one check. Answers vary. Track a trend.
  • Blocking snippets by accident. A nosnippet or noindex setting limits how your content can be used.

A 30-day plan

Week 1: list and baseline

Pick 20 to 30 searches. Run them logged out and record which trigger an AI Overview and who is cited.

Week 2: fix eligibility and openings

Check indexing, status codes, robots.txt and snippet settings for your target pages. Move a direct answer to the top of each page.

Week 3: fill the gaps

Write one page for the most important follow-up question. Add a source and a date to your key claims. Plan one real story for a site that is already cited.

Week 4: measure and decide

Run the same checks again. Compare with week 1. Decide what to repeat. Then keep checking monthly.
