SaaS SEO is search engine optimization for software sold by subscription. Its job is to bring in buyers who are choosing a tool, and to turn them into trials, demos and customers. The best order is to start with the pages closest to a purchase, such as comparison, alternatives, integration and pricing pages. Then build content for earlier questions, fix the technical base and make sure AI search tools describe you correctly.

This guide gives a ten-step plan for B2B software companies. It covers keyword research by funnel stage, the page types that convert, technical problems that SaaS sites have, how AI search changes the work, how to measure results, and when to hire help. We cite only studies and documents we could read at the source, and we say when a number comes from a vendor's own client data.

What is SaaS SEO?

SaaS SEO is the practice of making a software company's site easy to find, understand and trust in search, for the questions that buyers ask while they decide what to buy. It covers the marketing site, the blog, the help center and any public pages of the product itself, such as templates and free tools.

How is SaaS SEO different from regular SEO?

The basics are the same: crawlable pages, clear content and good links. The differences come from how software is bought.

Why does SaaS SEO matter now?

Two sets of data explain the stakes. They come from different sources and have different limits.

  • Buyers research with AI. G2 surveyed 1,169 B2B decision-makers in April 2025 for its Buyer Behavior Report. It found that 79% said AI search had changed how they research, and 29% said they start research with AI search more often than Google. Generative AI chatbots were the top source influencing vendor shortlists, at 17.1%, ahead of software review sites at 15.1%. The sample is software buyers who answered G2's survey, so it may lean toward people who use review sites.
  • Organic search can convert well, on one vendor's data. First Page Sage, an SEO agency, published funnel benchmarks from more than 50 B2B SaaS clients over a decade, mostly companies with $10M to $100M in revenue. In that data, SEO visitors became leads at 2.1% and paid search at 0.7%. Of the marketing-qualified leads, 51% from SEO and 26% from paid search became sales-qualified leads. These are one agency's client numbers, with no sample size per row, and they assume strong execution. Use them as a rough guide, not a target.

Search itself is also changing. The Pew Research Center tracked 900 US adults in March 2025 and found that people clicked a regular result in 8% of visits to pages with an AI summary and 15% of visits to pages without one. Google disputes that study's method. The practical point is that being named in the answer matters as well as ranking under it.

A ten-step SaaS SEO plan

Step 1: Define who you sell to and what job they hire you for

Write down your ideal customer, the problem they have, the tools they use now and the reasons they switch. These notes feed every later step. The best source is your own sales calls, lost deals, support tickets and customer interviews, not a keyword tool.

Step 2: Do keyword research by funnel stage

Group keywords by what the buyer wants. The table shows page types and the query patterns they match.

StageBuyer questionPage typesExample query pattern
DecisionWhich vendor, and at what price?Comparison, alternatives, pricing, integration, use-case, customer story"[competitor] alternative", "[you] vs [competitor]", "[category] for [role]"
ConsiderationWhich kind of tool fits us?Category guides, buying checklists, "best" lists, templates"best [category] software", "[category] software for [use case]"
AwarenessWhat is this problem and how do I fix it?Guides, glossaries, research"how to reduce churn", "what is [concept]"

Decision-stage terms have low volume, and keyword tools often understate them, because buyers phrase the same need in many ways. Several agencies report that one comparison page ranks for hundreds of variants. Treat that as an agency observation. Check your own Search Console for queries with words like "vs", "alternative", "pricing" and "integration", and look at those with a position between about 8 and 30, where Google already links the page with the intent.

To choose what to build first, score each keyword on three things: does it match a problem your product solves, can you realistically rank for it now, and would a searcher be a qualified lead? Search volumes show why you must pick with care. In Semrush's US database on 15 November 2026, "saas seo" has about 2,900 searches a month at a difficulty of 34, while "seo for saas" has about 1,300 at 33 and "saas seo strategy" about 590 at 27. Agency terms such as "saas marketing agency" run higher but are won by agencies and review sites, not by product pages.

How do you find low-competition, high-intent SaaS keywords?

Use four sources, in this order. Each one finds terms that a generic keyword list misses.

  1. Your competitors' money pages. Put your three or four closest rivals into a keyword tool and filter their ranking pages for "vs", "alternative", "best", "pricing" and "for". That list shows what they have judged worth building.
  2. Your own conversations. Sales calls, lost deals, cancellation notes and support tickets give the exact words buyers use. No tool has these.
  3. Your product. Each feature, integration and use case on your own site is a possible page. An audit of your product pages often turns up dozens of candidates.
  4. Search Console. Filter queries for the same words and look at those where you already appear but rank below the top results.

Then check difficulty in a tool and the search results themselves. If the top results are large review sites and big software brands, aim for a narrower phrase, such as a use case or an integration, where a focused page can win.

Step 3: Build the bottom-of-the-funnel pages first

These pages meet buyers when they are ready to choose. Build them in this order:

  • Alternatives pages for the competitors your buyers leave. Name the usual reasons, such as price, missing features or team size, and show which option suits which reason, including options that are not you.
  • Versus pages for two-name shortlists. Put a verdict near the top and a dated comparison table.
  • Integration pages for each tool your product connects to. Say what syncs, in which direction, how often, on which plans and how to set it up.
  • Use-case pages for each role or industry you serve, with real workflows and proof, not swapped nouns.
  • A pricing page that states prices or the factors that set them. A page that hides everything behind "contact sales" gives search engines and AI tools little to quote.

Be accurate and fair. Every claim about a competitor must be true on the day you publish, and you must date it. A comparison that admits where the rival wins is more believable and more likely to be quoted.

Step 4: Add problem and category content

Once the decision pages exist, write the guides and checklists that serve earlier questions. Group them into clusters around a few core topics, with one main page for each and supporting pages that link to it and to each other. Write for people first. Google warns that making separate pages for every small variation of a question, mainly to manipulate rankings or AI answers, breaks its scaled content abuse policy.

Step 5: Use the product as a source of pages

Templates, calculators, free tools and public help articles can attract links and searches that a blog cannot. Keep them useful on their own, and link them to the right product page.

Step 6: Fix the technical base

SaaS sites run into the same problems again and again:

  • JavaScript rendering. If your marketing pages load their text in the browser, check the raw HTML. In December 2024, Vercel reported that none of the major AI crawlers it tested rendered JavaScript, and Google's own guidance says JavaScript sites need extra care. Server-render or pre-render your public pages.
  • App and marketing site on different hosts. Decide what belongs in search. Keep logged-in app pages out of the index, and keep your docs and help center crawlable.
  • Parameters, filters and duplicate pages. Use canonical URLs and keep low-value pages out of the index.
  • Speed and page experience on mobile and desktop.
  • Clean redirects and sitemaps when you ship a redesign or a new docs system.

Step 7: Earn links and mentions in an honest way

Links still matter for ranking, and mentions on other sites matter for AI answers. Good sources for a SaaS firm are integration partners, review sites such as G2 and Capterra, original research with real data, podcasts, and industry press. Do not buy reviews or links. Google says inauthentic mentions are not as helpful as they might seem.

Step 8: Plan for AI search

Many buyers now ask AI tools to build a shortlist, so the work above also needs to hold up there. Make sure the AI crawlers you want can reach your pages, keep your facts the same across your site and your profiles, and put short, sourced answers at the top of key pages. Then run a fixed list of buyer prompts each month and record whether you are named and cited. Step 9: Measure what matters to the business

Traffic and rankings are leading signs. Judge the program by trials, demo requests, qualified leads and pipeline from organic search. Track:

  • Visitor-to-lead and visitor-to-trial rates for each page type
  • Leads that become sales-qualified, by landing page
  • Impressions and clicks for decision-stage queries in Search Console
  • Impressions in Google's Generative AI performance report, and mentions across your prompt list
  • Branded search, as a sign of awareness

Compare yourself with your own past results first. Public benchmarks vary by source, and most come from a single vendor's clients.

Step 10: Refresh and repeat

Update comparison, pricing and integration pages whenever the facts change. Review the plan every quarter against pipeline, not only against traffic.

Worked example: a small SaaS team reorders its plan

This is an illustration, not a client result. Imagine a 25-person company that sells invoice approval software to accounting firms. Its blog has 60 posts on general finance topics and gets visits, but almost no demo requests. Its site has no comparison page and no integration page.

The team lists the competitors named in its last 30 sales calls and the tools its customers connect, such as accounting packages. It plans eight pages: three alternatives pages, two versus pages and three integration pages, plus a rewrite of its pricing page. It writes each page in the plan above, with dated tables and named proof from two customers who agreed to be quoted. It pauses new blog posts for six weeks.

It then tracks, for each new page, impressions and clicks in Search Console, demo requests, and how many of those leads reach a sales call. After 90 days it compares the pages with the blog posts on demo requests per visit. The point is not a promised lift. The point is to move effort toward pages that serve buyers and to measure the result against the team's own history.

How do you measure SaaS SEO and build topical authority?

Pair two ideas. First, build topical authority: choose the few subjects your product owns, write one strong page for each, and support it with focused pages that link to it and to each other. Practitioners describe this as a pillar and cluster structure, and it also helps internal linking and AI answers. Second, measure business results by page type, not only organic traffic. A useful set is: impressions and clicks for decision-stage queries, visitors who start a trial or request a demo, leads that reach sales, the share of pipeline from organic search and the cost of the work against that pipeline. Review it each month with sales, since sales calls show which pages buyers read and which questions they still ask. If traffic rises and trials do not, the pages are attracting the wrong readers.

Should you hire a SaaS SEO agency or consultant?

You can start in-house if you have a writer, a developer with time for technical fixes and someone who talks to customers. Hire help when you lack one of those, when you need a technical audit, or when you want a plan before you spend on content. Ask for the deliverables: a keyword and page map by funnel stage, a list of technical fixes in priority order, the comparison and integration pages, and a report tied to trials and pipeline. We did not find independent data on typical fees or timelines, so ask for written quotes and be careful with promises of rankings.

How long does SaaS SEO take?

No independent study we found gives a reliable timeline. Decision-stage pages on a site that is already indexed can start to appear within weeks. New sites, competitive terms and link building take longer. First Page Sage reports about a seven-month break-even for B2B SaaS SEO, but that figure comes from its own clients and should be treated as a claim, not a rule. Judge progress by your own leading signs: impressions, then clicks, then trials.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Starting with a big blog. Traffic from general guides rarely turns into demos on its own.
  • Ignoring comparison and integration pages. They are low in volume and high in intent.
  • Chasing volume. A term with 50 searches from the right buyer can beat one with 5,000 from the wrong audience.
  • Unfair or stale comparisons. A false or outdated claim can hurt trust and invite complaints.
  • Hiding prices. Buyers and AI tools both look for them.
  • Letting the app and the site fight each other. Decide what belongs in the index.
  • Measuring only traffic. Tie the work to trials and pipeline.

Final thoughts

SaaS buyers choose slowly and check many sources, including AI tools. Meet them where the choice is made: with honest comparison, integration, use-case and pricing pages first, then guides, then the technical and AI search work that keeps everything findable. Measure by trials and pipeline, and keep the facts fresh.
