Generative engine optimization, or GEO, is the work of making your website easy for AI search tools to find, understand and quote. These tools include ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, and Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode. Instead of a list of ten links, they write one answer. GEO aims to get your brand named in that answer, ideally with a link.

This guide explains GEO in plain words. You will learn how AI tools pick sources, how GEO differs from SEO, what the research says, which tactics are worth your time and which are noise. It includes two worked examples, a first-week checklist and a 90-day plan. Our view is simple: GEO is built on good SEO, with a few extra habits on top.

What is generative engine optimization?

Generative engine optimization is the practice of improving your content and site so that AI systems use it when they write answers. A "generative engine" is any search tool that writes an answer instead of only listing links. The goal is not a rank. The goal is a mention, a quote or a citation inside the answer.

The term comes from a 2023 research paper by Pranjal Aggarwal and colleagues from Indian Institute of Technology Delhi, Princeton University, Georgia Tech and the Allen Institute for AI. It was later published at the KDD 2024 conference. The authors tested which changes to a page made an AI tool more likely to use it. They reported that the best methods raised visibility by up to 40% in their tests.

That number needs care. The tests used a research benchmark and a test setup, not your market. Treat it as a sign that content choices matter, not as a promise for your site.

You will see other names for the same work: AI search optimization, answer engine optimization (AEO), LLM optimization and "AI SEO". The names overlap. In practice they all ask the same question: when someone asks an AI tool about your topic, does it know you, trust you and quote you?

Why does GEO matter now?

People now ask questions in AI tools that they used to type into a search box. They ask longer, more detailed questions. They also get an answer without clicking. That changes what visibility means for a business.

Three changes make this a real business issue:

  • Answers replace lists. If an AI answer names three suppliers, the buyer may never see the ten blue links.
  • Brand mentions carry weight. A named recommendation from a tool a buyer trusts can start a shortlist.
  • Measurement is harder. Rankings tell only part of the story, so you need new checks (more on that below).

Here is what published studies show, with their limits:

  • Clicks fall when an AI summary appears. The Pew Research Center tracked the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025. Users clicked a regular result link in 8% of visits to Google pages with an AI summary and 15% of visits to pages without one. They clicked a link inside the summary in 1% of visits.
  • AI summaries are common for long questions. In the same study, 18% of all Google searches produced an AI summary. That share was 8% for searches of one or two words and 53% for searches of ten words or more.
  • Ranking is no longer the whole story. Ahrefs found that about 38% of the pages cited in Google AI Overviews ranked in the top 10 for the same query in March 2026, down from about 76% in July 2025.
  • Answers vary. SparkToro and Gumshoe found less than a 1 in 100 chance that ChatGPT or Google's AI would give the same list of brands twice for the same prompt.

These studies cover Google in particular months and a set of test prompts. They do not tell you how much traffic AI tools send to your site. We found no solid public figure for that, so we do not quote one. You can test where your own brand appears today.

How do AI search tools choose their sources?

Most AI search tools follow a similar loop. Google describes two parts of it for AI Overviews and AI Mode: retrieval-augmented generation and query fan-out.

  1. Understand the question. The tool reads the prompt. It often splits one question into several smaller searches. Google calls this query fan-out. A question like "how do I fix a lawn full of weeds" can become searches for weed killers, natural methods and prevention.
  2. Retrieve pages. The tool looks up pages and passages in a search index. It can only retrieve pages it has crawled and indexed.
  3. Read and select. The model reads the passages. It keeps those that answer the question clearly and agree with other trusted sources.
  4. Write and cite. The model writes its answer from those passages and links some of them.

Two lessons follow from this loop. First, if a tool cannot reach your page, nothing else matters. Second, because of fan-out, a page that answers one small sub-question well can be picked even when it does not rank first for the main question.

There is also a second source of knowledge: what the model learned in training. You cannot change that quickly. Over time, clear facts about your brand that appear in many trusted places help here.

What is the difference between GEO and SEO?

SEO aims to rank pages in a list of results. GEO aims to be named and cited inside a written answer. The foundation is shared: a page that cannot be crawled, loads slowly or says nothing useful will fail at both.

GoalA high position in resultsA mention or citation in an AI answer
Main unitThe page and the keywordThe passage, the fact and the brand
What helpsRelevance, links, technical health, page experienceThe same, plus quotable answers, clear facts and mentions elsewhere

Google is direct about this. Its own guidance says that, from its point of view, optimizing for generative AI search is still SEO. It also says a page must be indexed and eligible to show with a snippet to appear as a supporting link, and that there are no extra technical requirements.

So do you need a separate GEO program? Usually no. You need strong SEO first, then a few habits that make your answers easy to lift and your brand easy to recognize. If your site has crawl or indexing problems, fix those before anything else. Check robots.txt first, because it is the first gate.

What does Google itself say about GEO?

Google published guidance on how its AI features work and how to appear in them. The main points are worth knowing, because they cut through a lot of hype.

  • Use normal SEO. Crawlable pages, internal links, a good page experience and helpful content are the base.
  • Make non-commodity content. Google asks for content with a point of view and first-hand experience, not a summary of what everyone already says.
  • Keep structured data honest. It should match the visible text. It is not required for AI features, and no special markup is needed.
  • Ignore some "hacks". Google says you do not need llms.txt files, special chunking, rewriting only for AI, or fake mentions to appear in its AI features.
  • Measure in Search Console. Google reports AI feature traffic in Search Console and warns about tools that claim to use internal Google metrics.

One caution. This is Google's view of Google Search. Other tools, such as ChatGPT and Perplexity, have their own crawlers and rules. Some site owners choose to publish an llms.txt file for those tools. Google says it ignores the file, so it will neither help nor hurt there. Treat it as optional, and never as a substitute for good pages.

What helps a page get quoted?

Across the research and the guides we read, the same habits appear again and again. They also fit what the Aggarwal paper tested, such as adding citations, quotations and statistics to a page.

Answer first

Put a short, direct answer under each question heading. Make the first one or two sentences make sense on their own. That is the piece a tool can lift without the rest of the page.

Be specific and name your sources

Numbers, dates and named sources give a tool something it can check. A claim such as "most buyers prefer short pages" is easy to skip. A claim with a source and a year is easier to trust. Link to the original source, not to another blog that repeats it.

Use real questions as headings

People ask AI tools full questions. Headings that match those questions make your page easier to match. Pull questions from sales calls, support emails and the "People also ask" box.

Be clear about who you are

Use one brand name everywhere. Keep an accurate About page, real author bios and consistent facts: what you do, where you work, who runs the company. Add Organization, Article, FAQ and Person markup if it matches the page. It will not force a citation, but it removes doubt.

Cover a topic in depth

A cluster of pages that answer related questions gives fan-out searches more places to land. Google warns that making many near-duplicate pages just to catch every variation is spam. Write each page because a reader needs it.

Keep pages fresh

Update facts and examples on your best pages on a schedule. Show an honest "last updated" date. Old numbers are an easy reason for a tool to prefer a competitor.

Earn mentions that are real

Third-party articles, reviews, comparisons and directories that mention you give the tool a second source to match. Look at which pages AI tools cite for your topic, then ask whether you have a real story for those publishers. Buying mentions or faking them is not worth it, and Google says it does not help.

How do you do GEO step by step?

Work in this order. Each step builds on the one before it.

  1. Check access. Make sure Googlebot and the AI crawlers you want can reach your pages. Check robots.txt, your firewall, your CDN and any bot protection. A firewall that blocks every unknown bot can block the ones you want.
  2. Check indexing. In Search Console, confirm your key pages are indexed. A page that is not indexed in Google cannot be a supporting link in its AI features.
  3. Make the text readable. Important content should be in the page text, not hidden in images or loaded only by scripts that crawlers may not run.
  4. List the questions that matter. Write 15 to 25 questions your buyers ask. Group them by stage: learning, comparing, buying.
  5. Test them. Ask each question in ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google AI Mode. Record whether your brand is named, whether you are linked, who else is named, and which pages are cited. This is your baseline.
  6. Fix the pages that should win. Add a clear answer at the top, a named source or two, a short FAQ and an honest update date. Do this for your five most important pages first.
  7. Fill the gaps. Where the tools cite competitors or third-party pages and you have nothing, plan one useful page or one real outreach story.
  8. Clean up your brand facts. Make sure your name, services, location and leadership facts match across your site, your profiles and your listings.
  9. Measure and repeat. Run the same tests monthly, record the results, and update the pages that slip.

Worked example 1: a B2B software company

This is an illustration, not a client result. Imagine a company that sells invoice software to small accounting firms. It ranks on page two for "invoice software for accountants" and gets few leads from search.

The team lists 20 questions buyers ask, such as "what invoice software works with UK VAT" and "how do accountants bill clients on retainer". They test all 20 in four AI tools. The brand is named in 2 of 80 answers. Two competitors and a review site appear in most of them.

They find three problems. The pricing page text is inside a script and is thin. The VAT page is five years old and has no sources. Several third-party review sites list an old product name.

They fix these in order. They put pricing in plain page text. They rewrite the VAT page with an answer at the top, the tax authority's own guidance as a source and a clear update date. They ask the review sites to correct the product name. Then they publish two new pages for the retainer billing questions, written by the head of customer success, with real screenshots.

At the next monthly test they compare against the baseline. They do not expect a jump. They check whether the brand appears in more answers, whether the cited pages are theirs, and whether visits from AI tools show up in analytics. If nothing moves after three months, they look again at access, content depth and mentions, in that order.

Worked example 2: a local professional firm

Again, this is an illustration. Imagine a small law firm that handles tenant disputes in one city. Buyers ask AI tools questions such as "can my landlord keep my deposit" and "who can help with a tenant dispute in this city".

The firm tests 15 questions. For the general question, the tools quote the government's own pages and a national charity. The firm cannot beat those for that question, so it does not try. For the local question, the tools name two competitors and a directory that lists them.

The plan is narrow. The firm makes its Google Business Profile and directory listings match its site. It writes one clear page on deposit disputes in that city, with the real steps, the local court details and a note on when to call a lawyer. A partner signs it as author with a short bio. It asks the directory to add the firm.

The lesson is to pick fights you can win. A small firm will not out-rank a government site on a general question. It can be the clear local answer to a local question.

How do you measure GEO?

There is no single report for all AI tools. Use a mix of simple checks and keep them consistent.

  • Prompt tests. Run the same 15 to 25 questions every month in the same tools. Record whether you are mentioned, linked or missing, and who else appears. AI answers vary from one run to the next, so look at the trend, not one result.
  • Search Console. Google reports performance for its AI features there. Use it for the basics: indexing, queries and clicks.
  • Analytics. Look at referral visits from AI tool domains such as chatgpt.com or perplexity.ai, and at what those visitors do next.
  • Brand checks. Search for your brand and your leaders by name and read what the tools say. Note anything that is wrong and trace it to a source you can fix.
  • Business results. Track leads and the question "how did you hear about us". Ask new customers whether they used an AI tool. A short form field is enough.

Be careful with paid tracking tools. Some are useful for running prompts at scale. None can see inside Google's ranking systems, and Google warns against tools that claim to. Check any tool's advice against official guidance before you act on it.

What are the risks and limits of GEO?

GEO is a young field, and honest advice includes the limits.

  • Results are not guaranteed. No one controls what an AI tool writes. You can improve your odds, not buy a result.
  • Answers change. The same question can give different sources on different days. One good test proves little.
  • Tools differ. What helps in Google AI Mode may not help in another tool. Test in the tools your buyers use.
  • Shortcuts can backfire. Fake mentions, hidden text and mass-produced pages go against Google's spam policies and can hurt you.
  • Some answers send no clicks. A mention can still help a buyer remember you, but it is harder to track. Plan for that.
  • Research has limits. The 2023 study used a benchmark and test engines. Real results depend on your market and your competitors.

Common GEO mistakes

  • Starting with tools, not access. A tracking tool does not matter if crawlers are blocked.
  • Chasing llms.txt first. It is optional at best. Fix pages and facts first.
  • Burying the answer. A long introduction before the point makes a page harder to quote.
  • Claims without sources. Unsupported claims are easy for a tool to skip.
  • Writing for tools, not people. Google says to write for your audience. Pages that read like a keyword list help neither.
  • Inconsistent facts. Two different descriptions of your company across the web confuse people and tools.
  • One test, one conclusion. Run the same test over several months before you decide something works.
  • Ignoring SEO. If you are not indexed or you rank nowhere, GEO has little to work with.

Your first-week GEO checklist

  1. Confirm robots.txt, your firewall and your CDN allow the crawlers you want.
  2. Check that your top ten pages are indexed in Search Console.
  3. Write 15 to 25 buyer questions.
  4. Test them in four AI tools and save the answers, the cited pages and the date.
  5. Pick your five most important pages.
  6. Add a direct answer, one named source and an honest update date to each.
  7. Check that your company name, services and location read the same everywhere.
  8. Check that each article has a real author and bio.
  9. Look at your analytics for visits from AI tool domains.
  10. Set a monthly date to run the same tests again.

A 90-day GEO plan

Days 1 to 30: access and baseline

Fix crawling and indexing problems. Build the question list and run the first tests. Record the baseline. Fix facts that are wrong or out of date.

Days 31 to 60: improve and fill gaps

Rewrite your five most important pages. Add answers, sources and update dates. Write one or two new pages for questions where you are missing. Fix directory and profile details.

Days 61 to 90: earn and measure

Pitch one or two real stories to publishers that AI tools already cite for your topic. Rerun the tests. Compare against the baseline, note what moved and decide what to repeat.

A small team can do this alongside normal work. The point is a steady rhythm, not a big launch. We suggest a few pages per month rather than a burst, because quality and steady updates matter more than volume.
