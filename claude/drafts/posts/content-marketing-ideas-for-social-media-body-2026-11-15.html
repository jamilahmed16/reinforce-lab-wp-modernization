The best social media post ideas teach something useful, show how you work, prove your results, start a conversation and, now and then, ask for the sale. Short video is the format people say they interact with most on most networks, but one idea is not enough. A steady mix of five kinds of posts, matched to the platform your customers use, beats chasing every trend.

This guide gives you 30 ideas grouped by the job each post does, current data on platforms and what people want from brands, a simple content mix, and a 30-day plan. It replaces our older post on the same topic. We cite only sources we could read at the publisher, and we say where a survey is run by a software vendor.

What is content marketing on social media?

It is publishing useful, entertaining or helpful posts, videos and stories on social platforms so that the right people follow you, trust you and take a next step. It differs from paid ads, where you pay for each view. It also differs from a blog, where people arrive from search. On social media, people find your post because it appears in a feed, so the first seconds matter.

The aim is not likes. It is a clear result: visits to your site, messages, sign-ups, bookings or sales. Pick that result before you pick the idea.

What does the data say about social media in 2026?

  • Where people are. Pew Research Center's June 2026 survey of US adults found that 84% use YouTube, 70% Facebook and 51% Instagram. TikTok was used by 37%, LinkedIn by 31%, Snapchat by 26% and X by 18%. Pew's 2025 report, which surveyed 5,022 adults, found that 80% of 18 to 29 year olds use Instagram, against 19% of adults 65 and older. YouTube and Facebook are the only major sites that most people in every age group use.
  • What people engage with. Sprout Social's 2026 content strategy report surveyed over 2,300 consumers in the US, UK and Australia. It found that short video under 60 seconds is the brand content people are most likely to interact with on TikTok (60%), Instagram (52%), Facebook (48%) and X (37%). On YouTube, short and long video were almost level (52% and 49%). On LinkedIn, text posts led at 51%. Sprout is a software vendor, so read this as survey data, not a law.
  • What people want from brands. In Sprout's Q1 2026 pulse survey of 2,250 social media users, educational posts about products or services were the top request at 40%, then community-focused content (27%), episodic series (20%), behind-the-scenes content (19%) and memes and skits (18%). More people wanted content from front-line employees (16%) than from executives (9%). The one thing consumers most wished brands would stop doing was posting AI-generated content without labels.

The takeaway: teach first, show the people behind the work, keep videos short and be honest about how you made the content.

What is a good content mix?

Think of five jobs. Each post should do one of them clearly:

  • Teach: help people do or understand something.
  • Show: let people see how you work and who you are.
  • Prove: show results, reviews and customer stories.
  • Join: start a conversation and reply to it.
  • Sell: make a clear offer.

As a starting point, many teams give most of their posts to teaching and showing and keep selling to a minority. This is a rule of thumb, not research. Adjust it after a month of your own results.

What are social media content pillars, and how many do you need?

Content pillars are the few themes you return to again and again. They keep your feed focused and make planning faster. Most teams use three to five. We found no reliable benchmark, so start with the five jobs above and adjust after a month of your own results.

30 social media post ideas (content marketing ideas by job)

Teach: ideas that build authority

  1. Answer your most common customer question. Use a short video or a carousel. Put the answer in the first line.
  2. Share a how-to in five steps or fewer. One task, one post. A step-by-step carousel works well.
  3. List three mistakes people make. Make them specific to your trade, not generic.
  4. Bust a myth. State the belief, then show the truth in one clear cut or slide.
  5. Explain a term. Take one piece of jargon and define it in plain words.
  6. Post a checklist people can save. Seasonal checklists work well.
  7. Compare two options. Explain who each suits and when the other is better.
  8. Share one useful number. Use a statistic you read at the source and say where it came from.

Show: ideas that build connection

  1. Go behind the scenes. Show the process, not a blurry photo of a desk.
  2. Introduce a team member. Include their role, a useful tip and one personal detail.
  3. Share a day in the life. Fifteen to thirty seconds is enough.
  4. Tell why you started. A short, honest founder story.
  5. Show a mistake and what you learned. It builds trust.
  6. Spotlight front-line staff. Sprout's survey shows people want to see them more than executives.
  7. Preview a launch or project. Share work in progress and ask a question.

Prove: ideas that build trust

  1. Tell a customer story. Problem, what you did, result. Get permission first.
  2. Show a before and after. Real photos only, labeled with the time it took, and not presented as typical unless it is.
  3. Share a review with context. Add the story behind it.
  4. Feature customer content. Repost with permission and credit.
  5. Turn a case study into a carousel. One result per slide.
  6. Share a milestone and what it took. Tell what you learned, not only that you grew.

Join: ideas that start conversations

  1. Run a poll. Ask about a real choice you face, then tell people what you decided.
  2. Host a live question session. Announce it ahead and take questions in advance.
  3. Ask for advice. People like to be asked.
  4. Start a weekly series. A named series, such as "Friday fix," is easier to keep up.
  5. Reply to comments with a video. Turn a good question into a post.

Sell: ideas that drive action

  1. Show the product in use. Show it solving a real problem.
  2. Answer a pricing or delivery question. Clear answers sell.
  3. Announce an offer with a clear deadline. Keep it honest, with no fake urgency.
  4. Offer a free resource. Link to a guide, template or checklist that leads to your service.

How do you find content ideas that fit your audience?

  • Use your own analytics. Look at which posts got saves, shares, replies and clicks, not only likes.
  • Read your inbox and comments. Customers tell you what to post about.
  • Ask your sales or support team. The questions they hear most make the best posts.
  • Study competitors and peers. Note what gets real replies, then do your own version with your own proof.
  • Write a one-paragraph customer profile. Who they are, what worries them and where they spend time.
  • Use a research tool. Platform search boxes, Google's related searches and, if you have it, Semrush or a similar tool show what people ask.

How do you adapt one idea for each platform?

Do not post the same file everywhere. Keep the idea and change the format. One customer question can become:

  • a 30-second video for Instagram Reels, TikTok and YouTube Shorts,
  • a text post with three bullet points for LinkedIn,
  • a carousel for Instagram and LinkedIn,
  • a short post with a photo for Facebook,
  • a blog post or page on your site that the posts link to.

This is repurposing. It saves time and makes your best idea work harder.

Should you follow trends?

Only when it fits. Before you join a trend, ask four questions: Does my audience know it? Does it fit my tone? Can I add something original? Will the post still make sense next month? Skip trends tied to sensitive news or causes unless you have a real connection to them. A trend you force can cost trust.

What are the rules for sponsored and customer content?

  • Disclose paid or free-product relationships. The FTC's Endorsement Guides say a material connection between an endorser and a seller must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously. That includes payment, free or discounted products and early access. Put the disclosure on the post itself, in the same medium, where people will notice it.
  • Do not fake reviews or followers. The FTC's 2024 rule on reviews and testimonials covers fake reviews and fake social media indicators. AI-written reviews are included.
  • Get permission before you feature a customer's name, photo, words or video.
  • Label AI-generated content. In Sprout's pulse survey, unlabeled AI content was what consumers most wanted brands to stop doing.
  • Follow each platform's rules for ads, giveaways and contests, and check the live policy before you run one.

How often should you post?

We found no reliable independent benchmark. Platform advice varies, and the best pace depends on your team. Choose a rhythm you can keep for three months, such as three to five posts a week on your main platform, and keep it. Quality and a steady schedule matter more than volume. If you have to cut something, cut the number of platforms before you cut the quality.

How do you test whether an idea works?

  1. Pick one idea and one result, such as saves, clicks or replies.
  2. Post it in two or three formats over a few weeks.
  3. Compare results against your own recent average.
  4. Keep what beats it. Drop what does not. Repeat what worked with a new example.

Judge each format by its own job. A tutorial may earn saves, a customer story may earn profile visits, and an offer may earn clicks. Do not judge them all by likes.

How do you build a content calendar?

A content calendar is a simple table that shows what you will post, where and when. It keeps you from scrambling each morning, and it helps you balance the five jobs. Build one in a spreadsheet:

  1. List the dates for the next four weeks, down the left side.
  2. Add a column for each platform you use.
  3. Fill in one idea per slot and tag it teach, show, prove, join or sell.
  4. Add the format and a hook for each, such as "30-second video: the mistake we see most."
  5. Mark seasonal dates and your own launches first, then fill the gaps.
  6. Leave two open slots a week for timely posts and replies to comments.

Review the calendar every month against your results, and move the formats that worked into more slots.

How do you write captions and hooks that get read?

Most people decide in the first seconds whether to keep watching or reading. Open with the point. Say who the post is for or what they will learn, then give it. Keep captions short for video and add the detail in text on the screen. Use plain words, put the key phrase early, and add captions to every video, since many people watch without sound. Add one clear next step, such as "save this" or "ask us in the comments," and use only a few relevant hashtags. Quotes, images and memes can add variety, but they work best when they carry your own point of view, not a borrowed line.

A 30-day plan

  1. Week 1: Set up. Pick one or two platforms and one result. Write a one-paragraph customer profile. List 20 ideas from this guide and your inbox.
  2. Week 2: Teach and show. Post two teaching posts and two behind-the-scenes posts. Reply to every comment within a day.
  3. Week 3: Prove and join. Post one customer story and one result with permission. Run one poll and one live question session.
  4. Week 4: Sell and review. Post one clear offer. Then review the month, keep the top three formats and plan the next one.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Posting only promotions. People follow for value, not ads.
  • Using the same post on every platform. Change the format for each.
  • Chasing trends that do not fit. It can look forced.
  • Ignoring comments. Conversation is part of the content.
  • Judging by likes. Saves, shares, replies and clicks tell you more.
  • Posting unlabeled AI content. People notice and dislike it.
  • Quitting after a month. Results build slowly. Keep a baseline and compare.

Worked example (illustrative, not a client result)

Take a hypothetical bakery with two staff. It picks Instagram and Facebook and one result: pre-orders. In week one, it posts a 20-second video answering "How far ahead should I order a cake?" and a photo of the team at 5 a.m. It adds a carousel on "3 mistakes when ordering a wedding cake," and a customer story with permission. It runs a poll on next month's flavor and tells people what won. Each week it checks saves, replies and pre-orders against its recent average. No numbers are claimed, because this is a template. The point is the mix: teach, show, prove, join and a small amount of sell.

Social media content ideas for small business

A small business has little time, so it needs ideas that are cheap to make and easy to repeat. Start with these, drawn from the 30 ideas above:

  • Answer one customer question each week in a short post or video (Teach).
  • Show the work: a job in progress, a packed order, a day in the shop (Show).
  • Share one real review or result, with permission (Prove).
  • Ask a simple question and reply to every answer (Join).
  • Make one clear offer a month, with a date and a link (Sell).

Pick two content pillars, post three times a week, and keep a one-page calendar. A repeatable plan beats a long list of ideas you never use.

Final thoughts

Good social content is not a stream of clever posts. It is a steady habit of being useful, showing real work and answering people. Pick one or two platforms, use the five jobs as a guide, keep videos short, label AI, and measure results that matter to your business.
