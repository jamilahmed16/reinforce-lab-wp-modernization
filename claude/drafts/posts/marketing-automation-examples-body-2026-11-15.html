Marketing automation examples are rules that send the right message or take the right action when something happens: someone signs up, leaves a cart, downloads a guide, books a demo or goes quiet. The most common are welcome series, abandoned cart emails, lead nurturing, lead scoring with sales alerts, post-purchase follow-up and re-engagement. AI does not replace these rules. It helps with drafting, scoring and personalizing inside them, and a person still decides the offer, the limits and what gets sent.

This guide gives twelve examples, each with its trigger, steps, timing, what AI can add and what to measure. It also covers what to automate without AI, what the evidence says, how to build and measure a flow, and the legal basics. We cite only sources we could read at the publisher, and we say when a figure comes from one vendor's customers.

What is marketing automation?

Marketing automation is software that runs marketing tasks by rules. A trigger starts a workflow, such as "new subscriber", "cart left", "form submitted" or "no purchase in 90 days". The workflow then waits, sends messages, updates records, scores leads or alerts a person. It works across email, SMS, web, CRM and chat.

When people search for "AI marketing automation", they usually mean the same workflows with AI added in some steps. The table shows the difference.

What does the evidence say about automated flows?

The best public data comes from email platforms, and it has limits. Three points help set expectations.

  • Automated flows earn more per message than one-off campaigns. Klaviyo's 2026 benchmarks, based on more than 183,000 of its customers, report that flows generated nearly 41% of email revenue from 5.3% of sends, and that revenue per recipient was nearly 18 times higher than for campaigns. Flows had a click rate of 5.58% against 1.69% for campaigns, and a placed order rate of 2.11% against 0.16%. Limits: these are Klaviyo customers, mostly online stores, and revenue is attributed by Klaviyo's own method.
  • AI recommendations may lift clicks. The same report says AI product recommendations lift email click rates to 3.75% on average. It is vendor data on one feature, so test it on your own list.
  • Most carts are abandoned. The Baymard Institute averaged 50 published studies from 2006 to 2025 and found a documented cart abandonment rate of 70.22%, with a range from 55% to 84%. It says the most useful benchmark is your own rate trending down. These are unweighted averages of different studies, so use them as a baseline.

Twelve marketing automation examples

1. Welcome series

Trigger: a new email subscriber or account. Steps: an immediate welcome with one clear action, a second email a couple of days later with your most useful content, a third with proof such as reviews or a case study, and a last one with the offer or next step. AI can add: a version of each email for the source of the signup, and subject line options to test. Measure: click rate, first purchase or first meeting, and unsubscribes.

2. Lead magnet delivery and nurture (B2B)

Trigger: a form for a guide or webinar. Steps: deliver the file at once, then send a related article after a few days, a case study after a week, and an invitation to talk. Stop the sequence when the person books a call. AI can add: a summary of the guide tailored to the person's role, and a short check of the job title against your ideal customer profile. Measure: replies, meetings and the share of leads that become sales-qualified.

3. Abandoned cart recovery

Trigger: items added to a cart with no checkout. Steps: vendor guides suggest a first reminder within one to three hours with the product image, a second email a day later with reviews or help, and a final message with a small incentive if your margin allows. Exit the flow when the person buys. AI can add: product recommendations when the item is gone, and wording tuned to the product. Measure: recovered orders, revenue per recipient and discount cost. Timings are vendors' suggestions, so test your own.

4. Browse abandonment

Trigger: a visit to a product page without adding to the cart. Steps: a light reminder a few hours later with the item and related options. Intent is lower than for a cart, so avoid heavy discounts. AI can add: related-product choices. Measure: click rate and later purchases.

5. Post-purchase follow-up and review request

Trigger: an order. Steps: a confirmation with next steps, tips on using the product after a few days, a request for honest feedback after delivery, and a suggestion for related products later. AI can add: tips matched to what was bought. Measure: review rate, repeat purchase and support tickets. Ask for reviews the same way for every buyer, with no reward tied to a positive rating.

6. Onboarding emails for software

Trigger: a trial signup. Steps: a quick-start email, a feature email based on what the user has not tried, a success story, and a nudge at the end of the trial. AI can add: a choice of help content based on product usage. Measure: activation rate and trial-to-paid conversion.

7. Lead scoring with a sales alert

Trigger: actions such as pricing page visits, repeated email clicks and form fills. Steps: add points for each action, subtract points over time, and when the score passes a set level, alert the owner with the lead's history. AI can add: a read of free-text answers and a one-line reason for the score. Measure: the share of alerted leads that become opportunities. Review the rules monthly against real outcomes.

8. Demo reminders and no-show follow-up

Trigger: a booked meeting. Steps: a confirmation, a reminder the day before and an hour before, and a polite rebooking message if the person does not attend. AI can add: a short agenda drafted from the booking form. Measure: show rate and rebooking rate.

9. Re-engagement of quiet contacts

Trigger: no opens, clicks or purchases for a set period. Steps: a "still interested?" email, a final offer or preference choice, and removal from the list if there is no response. This protects your sender reputation. AI can add: a prediction of who is worth another try. Measure: reactivation rate and the effect on complaints.

10. Renewal and milestone messages

Trigger: a subscription renewal date, an anniversary or a usage milestone. Steps: a notice before renewal with clear terms, a thank-you at a milestone, and a help offer when usage drops. AI can add: a flag for accounts that show signs of leaving. Measure: renewal rate and churn.

11. Event and webinar follow-up

Trigger: registration or attendance. Steps: a confirmation, reminders, then a recording and next step for attendees, and a recording and a softer invitation for those who missed it. AI can add: a summary of the session and answers to common questions, checked by the speaker. Measure: replies and meetings by group.

12. Internal routing from forms to the CRM

Trigger: a form submission. Steps: create or update the contact, tag the source, assign an owner, post an alert in Slack or email, and send a first reply. AI can add: a read of the message to judge topic and urgency. Measure: time to first response and the share of leads with a complete record. What should you automate without AI?

Many flows need no AI at all. Confirmations, reminders, delivery of files, simple welcome series, cart reminders, receipts and renewal notices work well with plain rules. Rules are easier to test, cheaper to run and easier to explain when something goes wrong. Add AI only where a rule cannot do the job, such as reading free text, writing a draft or picking a product to show.

Where does a person still decide?

  • The offer and the promise. What you claim, discount or guarantee.
  • Anything sensitive. Health, money, legal and complaint topics.
  • The first batch of AI drafts. Review them before sending, and widen freedom only where errors are rare.
  • Rules for who may be contacted. Consent, opt-outs and local law.
  • The reading of results. A person decides what a change in numbers means.

How do you build and measure a flow?

  1. Pick one goal for the flow, such as first purchase or a booked call.
  2. Define the trigger and the exit. Decide when someone enters, and when they leave, for example on purchase.
  3. Write the steps and timing. Keep the first version short.
  4. Set what AI may do. List the tasks, the review step and what it must never do.
  5. Test before launch. Send test messages, check links and merge fields, and check on mobile.
  6. Launch to a small group and watch results and complaints.
  7. Review each month and change one thing at a time.

Klaviyo defines revenue per recipient as total revenue generated divided by the total number of messages sent, and says that how it changes over time matters more than a single number. That is a useful measure for any flow: compare the revenue or meetings from a flow with the messages it sends, and compare it with your own earlier results.

A short checklist helps before any flow goes live. Does it have one goal? Is the exit rule set? Has a person read every message and checked every link? Is there an opt-out in each marketing email, and is the postal address in the footer? Is the sending domain authenticated? Is there a way to pause the flow quickly if something goes wrong? A flow that passes these checks is far less likely to cause damage while you learn what works.

Is AI marketing automation affordable for small businesses?

Often yes, at a small scale. Many email and CRM platforms have free plans or trials, and basic flows such as a welcome series or cart reminder are built in. We found no independent data on typical costs, and prices change, so check each vendor's current page. Start with one platform and two or three flows, add AI features only where they save real time, and track the result before you spend more.

What does the law require?

This is general information, not legal advice. In the US, the FTC says the CAN-SPAM Act covers all commercial email, with no exception for business-to-business email, and that each violating email can carry penalties of up to $53,088. The main rules are accurate sender information, honest subject lines, a clear ad disclosure, your postal address, a clear way to opt out, and honoring opt-outs within 10 business days. If a company sends email for you, both of you may be responsible. Rules in the EU and UK often require consent for marketing email and have strict rules on personal data, so take advice for your markets. Keep a suppression list that every tool checks.

Worked example: a small online store

This is an illustration with assumed numbers, not a benchmark. Imagine a store with 2,000 carts created a month. At the documented average abandonment rate of about 70%, around 1,400 carts are abandoned. The store builds a three-email cart flow: a reminder after one hour, reviews and help after a day, and free shipping after two days. It adds a welcome series for new subscribers and a post-purchase review request.

If the cart flow brought back 5% of abandoned carts, that would be 70 orders a month. The 5% is our assumption for the example, not a published figure. The store tracks the real number, and compares revenue per recipient for each flow with its campaigns. It uses AI to draft product tips for the post-purchase email, and a person approves the drafts. After three months it keeps the flows that pay for themselves and removes those that do not.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Automating a bad process. Fix the offer and the experience first.
  • Too many messages. Every flow adds to the load on each subscriber.
  • No exit rule. People who bought still get cart reminders.
  • Unreviewed AI content. Wrong facts and tone harm trust.
  • Ignoring consent and opt-outs. It is a legal and reputation risk.
  • Measuring opens only. Privacy features make opens unreliable. Track clicks, orders and meetings.
  • Never reviewing. Flows go stale. Check them monthly.

Final thoughts

Start with the simple, proven flows: welcome, cart, nurture, scoring and follow-up. Use plain rules where they work, add AI where it saves real effort and keep a person in charge of offers, limits and sensitive topics. Measure each flow against its goal and review it every month.
