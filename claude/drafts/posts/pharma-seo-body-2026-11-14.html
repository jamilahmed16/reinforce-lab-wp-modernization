Pharma SEO, also called pharmaceutical SEO or life sciences SEO, is search engine optimization for pharmaceutical and life sciences companies. It helps patients, caregivers, healthcare professionals and business buyers find accurate, approved information about conditions, medicines, research and suppliers. It works inside strict advertising rules, so the plan and the approvals have to be built together.

SEO is one part of pharma digital marketing, which also includes paid search, email, social media and field teams. This guide covers the organic search part. It explains what pharma SEO is, how it differs from ordinary SEO, which rules shape it, and how to run it in eight steps. It includes worked examples, a 90-day plan, questions to ask a pharma SEO company and the mistakes that cost teams the most time. It is not legal advice. Your medical, legal and regulatory team makes the final call on every claim.

What is pharma SEO?

Pharma SEO means making regulated health and company information easy to find in Google and in AI answers, without weakening the evidence or the review process behind it. The mechanics are the same as in any SEO: pages, links, technical health and content that answers real questions. What changes is who owns each page, which statements are allowed and how long a change takes to approve.

It covers four kinds of site:

  • Manufacturer and brand sites. Corporate pages, pipeline pages, product information and disease education.
  • Contract and supply sites. Contract manufacturers, active ingredient suppliers, packaging and testing firms that sell to other companies.
  • Pharmacy and distribution sites. Wholesalers, specialty pharmacies and online pharmacies.
  • Research and education sites. Trial information, medical education and patient groups.

What is the difference between branded and unbranded search?

Branded searches include a product or company name. Unbranded searches are about a condition, symptom or treatment class and do not name a brand. They play different roles.

  • Unbranded search builds trust early. A patient who reads a clear explanation of a condition may remember the source.
  • Branded search is mostly navigation. People look for prescribing information, patient resources or contact details.

The link between the two needs care. For prescription medicines, disease education that links straight to product promotion can change how regulators view the education page. Treat internal links on these pages as a compliance question, and ask your regulatory team how the two page types may connect.

How to do pharma SEO: 8 steps

Step 1: Give every search one page owner

List the searches you want to win. For each one, name the single page that should answer it, the audience and the page type. Service pages, guides, glossary entries and product pages can all stay live, but each needs a clear job. If Search Console shows five pages getting impressions for the same phrase, Google cannot tell which one is meant to answer.

Step 2: Map your audiences and markets

Choose the audiences from the table above that matter to your business. Note the country for each. A page for UK patients, a page for EU professionals and a page for US prescribers are three different pages with three different review paths.

Step 3: Build a keyword map by intent

Use a keyword tool and your own Search Console data. Group the searches by audience and by what the searcher wants: to learn, to compare or to contact. Add a column for who must approve the page. Do not target a keyword on a page that cannot say what searchers want to know, for example a price or a claim that is not allowed.

Step 4: Design the page before you write it

Review can remove risk from a page. It cannot give a page a purpose it never had. Before drafting, fix these fields for each page:

  • Audience and market
  • The one question the page answers
  • Claims it will make, and the source for each
  • Approved wording and required safety text
  • Author, reviewer, owner and next review date

Put the answer at the top in two or three plain sentences. Then add detail.

Step 5: Bring SEO into the review process early

Many teams run review first and add SEO after. That turns a small title change into another approval round. Instead, share the keyword map and the page design with medical and regulatory colleagues before drafting, so wording is agreed once. Plan production around real approval times, publish durable pages first and keep a log of approved text that can be reused. Some agencies report approval cycles of several weeks. Check your own, because they vary by company and by content type.

Step 6: Fix the technical basics

  • Crawling and indexing. Make sure public pages can be crawled and indexed. Gate professional-only content clearly, and do not rely on gated pages as landing pages.
  • Speed and mobile. Professionals often search on a phone between appointments.
  • Site structure. Group pages by condition, audience and product, and link them in a logical path.
  • Titles and headings. Use the words your audience uses. Check each title for claims.
  • Structured data. Schema markup tells machines what a page is, such as an article, an organization or a medical web page. Google documents the types it supports. Use markup to describe the page honestly, and do not expect it to lift rankings by itself.
  • Images. Add plain alt text, and check that images do not imply a claim.

Step 7: Show evidence people can check

Authority comes from what a reader can verify. On every page show:

  • The author, with relevant experience
  • The medical or scientific reviewer, with the review date
  • Links to the sources for each important statement, preferring primary sources such as regulators, guidelines and peer-reviewed papers
  • A visible updated date, with changes logged when guidance changes
  • The scope: who the page is for and what it does not cover

Label worked examples as real, anonymized or illustrative. That does not weaken a page. It stops the page from promising evidence it does not have.

Worked example 2: a contract manufacturer

This example is also illustrative. A contract manufacturer of sterile injectables sells to other companies and has one general "services" page.

  1. Audit. The page is indexed but ranks for nothing, because it lists capabilities in one paragraph.
  2. Keyword map. Four searches: sterile injectable contract manufacturing, fill and finish services, small batch clinical supply, and the company name.
  3. Pages. One capability page for each, with equipment lists, batch sizes, quality certifications the company actually holds, an anonymized project example and a contact form.
  4. Evidence. Named quality head as reviewer, certification numbers and dates, and a changelog.
  5. Measure. Enquiries from organic search tagged by page and tracked into the sales system.

The result is a site that answers a buyer's detailed questions before the first call. Search volume for this kind of term is small, but each visit comes from a qualified buyer.

How do you write titles and descriptions in a regulated field?

Titles and meta descriptions are the first words a searcher reads, and they are short. That makes them easy to get wrong. A few rules keep them safe and useful:

  • Describe the page, do not promote. "Understanding [condition]: symptoms, causes and when to see a doctor" is safer than a line about results.
  • Avoid benefit words for products. Words such as "effective" or "proven" can read as claims. If a product is named, ask what risk information must sit with it.
  • Name the audience. "For healthcare professionals" in a title sets the right expectation and filters out the wrong clicks.
  • Keep them stable. Once approved, a title should not change often, because each change may need review.

How should internal links work?

Internal links tell Google which pages matter and how topics connect. In pharma they also shape how a regulator or reviewer reads a page. Plan them with the same care as the text.

  • Link disease education pages to each other and to related patient resources.
  • Link professional pages to evidence, trial and prescribing resources, not to patient pages.
  • For prescription products, agree with your regulatory team whether and how education pages may point to product pages.
  • Use clear link text that says where the link goes. Avoid "click here".
  • Write the linking rules down, so new pages follow them without a new debate.

How do you handle content for healthcare professionals?

Clinical data pages are valuable for authority, and they carry the highest accuracy risk. Some companies place them behind a professional check. That is a legitimate choice, but it has an SEO cost: search engines cannot read gated pages.

A workable pattern is to keep a public, indexable page that explains what the resource is, who it is for and what it covers, and to gate only the material that requires it. The public page can rank and send the right visitors to the next step. Make sure the public text follows the same approval rules as any other page.

What about pharmacies and distributors?

Pharmacies and distributors often win with local and trust signals more than with long articles. Check these basics:

  • A complete Google Business Profile with the correct name, address, phone number and hours
  • Licence and registration details on the site, in plain view
  • Pages for each service, such as repeat prescriptions or delivery, written in plain language
  • Clear information on what the pharmacy can and cannot advertise about prescription-only medicines in its country
  • Reviews that you ask for fairly and answer without sharing any patient detail

Can pharma companies use AI to write content?

Google has said that using AI is not against its guidelines when the result is helpful and made for people, and that using it mainly to manipulate rankings breaks its spam policies. For pharma the bigger point is accuracy and responsibility. An AI tool can invent a dose, a study or a safety statement that sounds right. Your company is responsible for every claim on its pages.

If you use AI in drafting, keep these rules:

  1. Give it approved source material, not open questions.
  2. Have a qualified person check every fact against the source.
  3. Run the normal medical, legal and regulatory review.
  4. Name the human author and reviewer on the page.
  5. Keep a record of the sources used.

Pharma SEO and AI search

People now ask AI tools and Google's AI answers about conditions and medicines. These tools summarize pages, and they can get facts wrong. For pharma this is a risk as well as a chance.

  • Put the answer first. A short, direct opening is easy for people and for machines to use.
  • Keep facts consistent. Names, indications and safety wording should match across your pages and your listings.
  • Cite your sources on the page. Pages that show where each statement came from are easier to trust.
  • Check what AI tools say. Ask them the questions your audiences ask. Record any wrong or unsafe answer, and fix the source pages first.
  • Know the limits. No method guarantees that an AI tool will cite you, and regulatory rules still apply to your own pages.

How long does pharma SEO take?

Google says some changes show in hours and others take months, and it suggests waiting a few weeks before judging a change. Pharma adds approval time before anything goes live. Plan for six to twelve months before you judge the program, and for a few months before the first pages are live.

Should you hire a pharmaceutical SEO company?

Some teams run SEO in-house with medical writers and a technical lead. Others use an agency for strategy, technical work and reporting. Whichever you choose, the same questions apply. Ask any pharmaceutical SEO company or agency:

  • Which audiences and markets have you worked in, and which rules do you plan around?
  • How will you work with our medical, legal and regulatory team, and when does SEO enter the process?
  • Who writes and who reviews the content, and what are their qualifications?
  • How do you report results, and can you tie them to enquiries or other business goals?
  • How do you earn links? Google lists buying links to affect rankings as link spam.
  • How do you check AI answers about our products?
  • What is in the written scope, and how can we end the contract?

Be careful with anyone who promises a ranking date or guarantees approvals. Nobody controls Google, and your own regulatory team controls approvals. Common pharma SEO mistakes

  • Adding SEO after review. A small change then triggers a new approval round.
  • Mixing audiences. One keyword list for patients, professionals and buyers makes pages that serve none.
  • Gating the wrong pages. If professional content is the landing page, public searchers find nothing to read.
  • Writing benefit-led titles. A title or description can carry a claim.
  • Linking education straight to promotion. Ask your regulatory team first.
  • No author, reviewer or date. Health pages without them lose trust.
  • Copying competitor pages. Duplicate pages do not earn authority.
  • Letting facts drift. Out-of-date doses and indications are a risk for patients and for you.
  • Buying links. Short-term gain, long-term risk.

A quick checklist

  • One page owner for each target search
  • Audiences and markets listed, each page tied to one
  • Keyword map with a reviewer for every page
  • Page designs and approved wording agreed before drafting
  • Author, reviewer, sources and dates visible on every health page
  • Professional-only content clearly marked, and public pages indexable
  • Titles and descriptions checked for claims
  • Technical basics fixed and measured
  • AI answers about your key questions checked each quarter
  • Monthly report that ties search to business goals

Final thoughts

Pharma SEO works when search and regulatory teams plan together from the start. Pick the audience, name the page that owns each search, show evidence a reader can check and measure in stages. The first few pages take longest. After that, the approved models make every new page faster to publish and safer to maintain.
