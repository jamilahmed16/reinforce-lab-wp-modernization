A generative engine optimization agency helps your brand get found, understood and cited by AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google's AI Overviews. To choose a good one, look for a method you can inspect: a prompt list you approve, a baseline measured before work starts, named changes, honest limits and clear ownership of your data. Walk away from any firm that guarantees citations or rankings, because no one controls AI answers.

This guide explains what these agencies do, whether you need one, twelve questions to ask with good and poor answers, red flags, how to compare proposals and what the cost depends on. What does a generative engine optimization agency do?

Generative engine optimization, or GEO, is work that improves how often and how accurately AI tools name your brand and cite your pages. Our guide to generative engine optimization explains the method. An agency usually sells some mix of these services:

  • A baseline audit. A fixed list of buyer questions run through several AI tools, with mentions, citations, descriptions and competitors recorded. Our AI search audit guide covers the method.
  • Technical fixes. Crawler access, indexing, rendering, structured data and site facts.
  • Content work. Answer-first pages, comparison pages, updates and sourced evidence.
  • Off-site work. Profiles, reviews, digital PR and presence on the sites the AI answers cite.
  • Measurement. Repeated prompt tests, Search Console and Bing reports, and AI referral traffic in analytics.

The names vary. Some firms say GEO, others AEO, LLM SEO or AI SEO. Google says that from its point of view, optimizing for generative AI search is still SEO, and it advises care with third-party "AEO" or "GEO" advice. So judge the work, not the label.

Do you need an agency at all?

Not always. The options are in-house, an agency or a tool. The table shows when each tends to fit.

Google's own advice on hiring an SEO applies here too. It says some sites do not need a hire, and it warns that no one can guarantee a number one ranking on Google, and to beware of firms that claim a special relationship with Google or a "priority submit".

What does the evidence say about GEO agencies?

Independent evidence is thin, and we found none on average fees. Four sources help you set expectations.

  • Agency case studies are often weak. OpenServed analyzed 142 GEO case studies published by 68 agencies. It found that 27% reported no AI metric, only 13% tied AI search to leads or revenue, and only 5 of 142 mentioned the prompts they tracked. None of the 142 stated a budget. The median result was measured over six months, and only 15% within three months. This is one directory's review of agency-published material, classified by its own method, so treat the figures as a rough picture.
  • No one can promise a result. Google says no one can guarantee a number one ranking, and Google says that no third-party tool has access to its internal ranking or AI systems.
  • Answers change. SparkToro had 600 volunteers run 12 prompts through ChatGPT, Claude and Google's AI features 2,961 times. There was less than a 1 in 100 chance of the same list of brands twice. A good agency reports a rate across many runs, not a rank.
  • Some measurement is free. Google's Search Console has a Generative AI performance report for AI Overviews and AI Mode impressions, and Bing Webmaster Tools has an AI Performance report for citations in Copilot. A good agency uses both in its baseline.

What are the red flags?

  • A guarantee of citations, rankings or traffic.
  • No baseline before the proposal.
  • Prompts that the agency chose and will not show, or that contain your brand name.
  • One AI tool only, or one screenshot as proof.
  • Reports that stop at mentions or a single score.
  • A price with no deliverables.
  • A claim of a special relationship with Google or any AI company.
  • Work only on your own site, with nothing on the sources the answers use.
  • A contract that locks your data and content in their platform.
  • Unreviewed AI-written content, especially in health, finance and legal fields.

Google adds a point worth remembering: you are responsible for what companies you hire do on your site. A deceptive tactic is your problem as well as theirs.

How much does a generative engine optimization agency cost?

We found no independent data on typical fees. Published ranges come from agencies' own pages and guides, and they disagree. OpenServed notes that price ranges in other guides are unsourced and conflict, and that none of the 142 case studies it reviewed states a budget. Instead of comparing monthly numbers, compare what you receive.

These things move a price:

  • How many AI tools and markets are tracked.
  • How many prompts are tested, and how often.
  • How much content is written, and by whom.
  • How much technical work your site needs.
  • Whether outreach and digital PR are included.
  • Whether tracking tool fees and CRM reporting are in scope.
  • How much of your team's time the work needs, for approvals and input.

GEO is usually sold in three ways: a one-time audit, a project such as a rebuild of key pages, or a monthly retainer. A fixed audit first is often the safest start. It gives you a baseline and a plan at a known cost, and you can use it to judge a longer program.

What should the first 90 days look like?

A sensible first quarter has a clear order. This is our recommendation, not a standard. Ask any agency to show its own version in writing.

PeriodWorkWhat you should receive
Weeks 1 to 2Agree the prompt list, competitors and success numbers. Run the baseline. Check crawler access and rendering.The prompt list, raw answers, a source map and a technical check
Weeks 3 to 6Fix access, facts and the few pages closest to buying questions. Claim and correct key profiles.A change log with dates and reasons
Weeks 7 to 10Publish or refresh answer-first pages with sourced evidence. Start outreach to the sources the answers cite.The pages, a list of sites approached and the status of each
Weeks 11 to 13Repeat the same prompt test and compare with the baseline. Review Search Console and Bing reports.A report that separates mentions from citations and says what else changed

If an agency cannot describe its first 90 days in this much detail, it probably does not have a repeatable method.

Should one firm run both your SEO and your GEO?

It often helps, because the two share a base. Access, indexing, content quality and links serve both, and a plan that treats them separately can send two teams to edit the same pages. If another agency runs your SEO, ask each firm to state who owns which pages and who approves changes. Be careful with any firm that sells GEO as a separate product and ignores SEO, or that asks you to drop a provider that is doing sound work. Google's own view is that its AI features rely on its core Search systems, so weak SEO tends to limit GEO.

How do you compare proposals?

Send every candidate the same request: a sample report, a case study with dates, a first-month plan, a list of owners and a proposed scope. Then score each on the same table.

CriterionAsk forWhat to check
MethodSample prompts, raw answers, citationsCan you inspect the scoring?
FitYour priority questions and competitorsDo the prompts reflect real buying decisions?
DeliveryOwners, approvals, access, change logCan changes ship and be checked?
ProofA dated case with a baselineAre limits and other changes stated?
TermsScope, exclusions, notice period, handoverDoes the fee match the work, and do you own the output?

Add the hours your own team will spend. A cheaper retainer that needs several hours a week from your marketing lead can cost more than a dearer one that runs on a short weekly review.

Worked example: a software company compares three proposals

This is an illustration, not a client case. Imagine a 40-person software company that sells to logistics teams. It asks three agencies for the same packet. Agency A sends a visibility score and a promise of top citations in 90 days. Agency B sends a baseline of 15 prompts it chose, several containing the company's name. Agency C asks for the company's sales call notes, builds 14 prompts from them with the company, and sends a baseline of 126 answers across three tools, with a map of the sites the answers cite and a list of eight site changes.

The company drops Agency A for guaranteeing a result and sending no method. It drops B because the prompts flatter the brand. It starts a fixed-price audit with C, owns the prompt list and the data, and decides on a retainer after reading the results. The choice did not depend on price. It depended on what could be inspected.

GEO consultant or agency?

A consultant is a good fit when you need a plan and an expert's review, and you have people to do the work. An agency fits when you also need the work done. In either case, ask the same twelve questions, and check who does the hands-on work.

How long does it take to see results?

Access and fact fixes can change retrieval-based answers within weeks. OpenAI says a robots.txt change can take about 24 hours to apply for ChatGPT search. Content and off-site work take longer. OpenServed found a median measurement period of six months in published case studies, but those are agency-chosen examples. Ask what the agency will count at day 30, day 90 and month six, and treat any promise of a date for citations as a red flag.

What should you own when the work ends?

Get these in writing: the prompt list, the baseline, access to or exports from the tracking tool, AI referral traffic tagged in your own analytics, and the pages and content created for you. Ask about the notice period and what happens to your data if you leave.

Which free checks can you run to test an agency's claims?

You can check much of what an agency tells you without paying for a tool. Open your Search Console and read the Generative AI performance report, which shows impressions in AI Overviews and AI Mode. Open Bing Webmaster Tools and read its AI Performance report for citations in Copilot. Run the agency's sample prompts yourself, three times in each tool, and compare your results with its screenshots. Check your robots.txt and your firewall for the bots it says it will manage. Ask for the prompt list behind any case study, and run those prompts today. If the agency says a page now ranks or is cited, check that it still does. An agency with a sound method will welcome these checks.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Choosing on price alone. A low fee for vague work can cost more than a clear scope.
  • Letting the agency choose the prompts alone. You know your buyers.
  • Accepting a score as proof. Ask for the answers behind it.
  • Paying a large retainer before a baseline exists.
  • Ignoring overlap with your SEO provider. Agree who does what.
  • Forgetting approvals. The fee runs on while drafts wait.

Final thoughts

The best generative engine optimization agency for you is the one whose method you can inspect. Ask for a baseline, a prompt list you approve, named changes, honest limits and clear ownership. Start with a fixed audit, compare proposals on deliverables and decide on a longer program after you have read the results.
