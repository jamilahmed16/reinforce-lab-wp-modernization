Content marketing for plastic surgeons means publishing useful, accurate material that helps a patient decide whether, when and with whom to have a procedure. That includes procedure pages, recovery guides, surgeon videos, consented before-and-after galleries, email follow-up and honest answers to cost and risk questions. It works because patients research for a long time before they book, and because health topics get extra scrutiny from search engines and from regulators.

This guide gives you 10 content ideas, the data on how patients choose a surgeon, and the privacy and advertising rules that apply in the United States. It replaces our older post on the same topic. We cite only sources we could read at the publisher, and we say where a study is small, old or based on an online survey. This is marketing guidance, not legal advice.

What is content marketing for plastic surgeons?

It is the practice of creating and sharing material that answers patient questions, so the right people find your practice, trust it and ask for a consultation. It is not the same as advertising. An ad pays for a view. Content earns attention because it is useful, and it keeps working for as long as it stays accurate.

For a plastic surgery practice, content usually falls into five groups:

  • Procedure pages that explain who a procedure is for, how it works, recovery, risks, results and cost factors.
  • Educational articles that answer one question each, such as "what does week two of recovery feel like?"
  • Video from the surgeon: introductions, explainers and live questions and answers.
  • Results and stories with written patient permission: galleries, case write-ups and reviews.
  • Email that follows up after an inquiry and after surgery.

How do patients choose a plastic surgeon? What the data shows

Three survey studies and one industry report give a useful picture. Each has limits, which we state next to the figure.

  • Demand is large and steady. The American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) counted 1,585,878 cosmetic surgical procedures in 2024, up 1% from 2023, and 28,243,407 minimally invasive procedures. Liposuction led surgery at 349,728 procedures, and neuromodulator injections led minimally invasive care at 9,883,711. ASPS says surgical counts come from member surgeons and are extrapolated from about 1,000 survey responses, with a 4.62 percent margin of error.
  • Google comes first, and social media matters too. A 2019 survey of 527 people, recruited online through MTurk, found that 46% would look on Google first for a plastic surgeon. A practice website was the most influential single online method (25%), but social platforms together ranked higher (35%). The same study found that 96% of respondents were unclear about which board certification a plastic surgeon should hold.
  • Results photos carry the most weight in feeds. In a 2021 survey of 401 people, before-and-after results drew the most interest of any content type. 45.9% named them the most influential part of a surgeon's social account, ahead of links to reviews (16.2%). It was also an online crowd sample, not a sample of patients.
  • Qualifications can matter more than followers. A 2021 national survey in Saudi Arabia of 7,190 people found surgeon qualifications the top factor (39.9%), then advice from friends and relatives (24%). The number of social media followers ranked last (0.8%). It used convenience sampling and a different market, so treat it as a counterweight, not a rule for the United States.

The common thread is that patients look for proof of training, real results and honest information. A 2026 journal review of plastic surgery marketing says patient material should put education first. It links educational social content to greater trust and a sense of control.

What rules apply to plastic surgery content?

Read this section before you plan a single post. A mistake here costs more than a weak headline.

Patient privacy: HIPAA

If your practice is a covered entity under HIPAA, using a patient's health information to promote your services is marketing. With limited exceptions, it needs the patient's written authorization. HHS says the Privacy Rule requires the patient's written authorization before health information is used for marketing. HHS also settled with a physical therapy provider. It had posted patient testimonials with full names and face photos on its website without valid authorizations.

In practice, this means:

  • Get a signed authorization for every before-and-after photo, video, quote or case story. A verbal yes, a text message or a line in the intake packet is not enough.
  • Say where the content will appear, for what purpose, and when the permission ends. Explain how the patient can withdraw it, and give them a copy.
  • Do not make care depend on signing.
  • Remember that cropped photos can still identify a person. Tattoos, scars, birthmarks and a few case details can be enough.
  • Do not reply to online reviews in a way that confirms the person was a patient.
  • Check what your marketing agency can see. An agency that handles patient information may need a business associate agreement.

A clinical photo taken for the chart is a treatment record. The moment you use it to attract patients, it becomes marketing. Ask your lawyer to approve your authorization form. HHS does not publish one mandatory form.

Truth in advertising and testimonials: the FTC

The FTC's Endorsement Guides say that when an ad shows a consumer's experience on a key attribute, people will read it as typical. If you cannot show that it is typical, you should clearly state what results people generally get. The guides also say not to distort reviews by suppressing or editing them in ways that misrepresent what customers think.

The FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (16 CFR Part 465) went further. It bans fake reviews and testimonials, including ones written with AI, along with buying reviews and offering incentives that depend on a positive review. When the FTC announced the rule in August 2024, civil penalties were up to $51,744 per violation. That figure is adjusted over time, so check the current amount. Do not buy reviews, write reviews for yourself or your staff without disclosure, or hide the bad ones.

Use the same discipline for results. Show a range of cases, label the procedure and time since surgery, and state that results vary.

Platform rules for paid ads

Meta's health and wellness advertising policy requires ads for cosmetic products, procedures and surgeries to be targeted to people 18 or older. The cached copy of the policy page that we read, dated 27 December 2024, lists before-and-after transformation imagery for general cosmetic procedures among its restricted items. A third-party summary says the rule was loosened in 2026, but we could not confirm that wording on Meta's own page. Check the live policy, and Google's healthcare ad rules, before every campaign.

Medical board, board certification and local law

State medical boards set their own advertising rules. Do not call yourself a "plastic surgeon" or "board certified" unless it is true and you can name the certifying board. A 2026 journal review notes that some providers who are not board certified market themselves as plastic surgeons. It urges board-certified surgeons to state their credentials and explain what they mean. If you practice outside the United States, check your own regulator and data protection law, since the rules above are US rules.

How Google treats health content

Google gives extra weight to trust, experience and expertise on topics that could affect health. It calls these "Your Money or Your Life" topics. Its helpful content guidance asks three questions: who created this, how was it created, and why. For a surgery practice, that means a named surgeon byline, a short bio with credentials, a clear review date, and content that exists to help the patient, not only to catch searches.

10 content ideas that work for plastic surgeons

1. A deep procedure page for each service you want to grow

Fix the page before you write the blog post. A good procedure page covers who is a good candidate and who is not. It explains the day of surgery, recovery by week, risks, how long results last and what affects cost. It ends with a clear next step. Add an FAQ and a short surgeon video. Add the surgeon's name, credentials and a review date. Every blog post about that procedure should link back to this page.

2. Recovery guides, week by week

Patients search for "what to expect" more than they search for the operation name. Write short, honest pages such as "What week two of tummy tuck recovery feels like." Use ranges. Say what is normal and what needs a call to the office. Have the surgeon review each one. Update them when your protocol changes.

3. Candidacy and risk content

This is the content many practices skip, and it builds the most trust. Explain who should not have a procedure, what the complications are, what a revision looks like and how often you see them, if you track it. Content that states limits as clearly as benefits filters out poor-fit inquiries and sets expectations before the consultation.

4. Before-and-after galleries with consent and context

Use the same lighting, background, angle and distance for every set. Label the procedure and the time since surgery. Show a range of body types and outcomes, not only your best cases. Never edit results. Turn the strongest cases into short case studies with the plan, the choice the patient made and the recovery, with the patient's written authorization on file.

5. Surgeon-led video

Patients choose a person. A two-minute introduction in which the surgeon explains their training and approach often does more than a long brochure page. Add short clips that each answer one question, such as "Am I too old for a facelift?" Add captions. Host the video on YouTube and embed it on the matching procedure page. Do not use AI tools to create or alter results imagery or to recreate a patient.

6. FAQs built from real consultation questions

Ask your patient coordinators for the 20 questions they answer most. Turn each into a short page or a section with a clear answer in the first two sentences. This format suits Google, and it suits AI answer tools that pull a direct answer from a page.

7. Comparison articles

Patients compare before they book: a mini facelift or a full facelift, surgical or non-surgical nose reshaping, implants or fat transfer. Write a fair comparison with a table of recovery time, longevity, risks and typical candidates. Say when the other option is the better choice. That honesty is what makes people trust the page.

8. Cost and financing explainers

Price is one of the first questions and one of the least answered online. You do not have to publish a fixed fee. Explain what drives cost, such as surgeon fee, facility, anesthesia and follow-up, give a range if you can, and describe financing options in plain words. Do not use urgency or "limited time" language.

9. Reviews and testimonials, handled lawfully

Ask every patient to review you on Google, in the same way, after care is complete. Do not choose who to ask based on how happy you think they are. Respond to reviews without confirming that the person was a patient. If you feature a testimonial on your site, get HIPAA authorization and follow the FTC rules above.

10. Email that follows the patient

Set up three short sequences. After an inquiry, send what to expect from a consultation and your surgeon's training. After a consultation, send answers to common questions and financing. After surgery, send recovery check-ins. Keep them clinical and kind. Include an unsubscribe link and your postal address, as the CAN-SPAM Act requires for commercial email, and avoid putting health details in the message body.

Which social platforms and paid channels fit?

Pick channels by who you want to reach and what format you can keep up. The 2026 journal review says Instagram suits younger people through photos, Reels and Stories. Facebook still serves older groups. TikTok reaches Gen Z and millennials through short, relatable video. In the 2021 survey, Instagram (71.1%) and Facebook (55.4%) were the platforms where people followed a plastic surgeon.

  • Instagram and TikTok: short surgeon answers, myth-busting, recovery explainers and consented results. Keep captions plain, and keep patient identity out of the frame unless you hold authorization.
  • Facebook and YouTube: longer education, live question sessions and embedded procedure videos that also support your website.
  • Google Ads: a fast way to reach people searching for a procedure now. Send each ad to the matching procedure page, and pause campaigns for procedures you cannot schedule soon. Check Google's healthcare ad rules first.
  • Reputation management: ask every patient for a Google review in the same way, and answer reviews without confirming the person was a patient.

If you do reconstructive work, the same review notes that growth there depends heavily on referring clinicians. Keep consumer content educational, and keep those referral relationships strong. Social channels amplify your pages. They do not replace a strong website.

How do you get found? Search, local and AI answers

  • Write for the search, not only for the keyword. Patients search for a procedure plus a place, and for questions such as cost, recovery and candidacy. Look at what ranks and build the page that answers the task better.
  • Keep your Google Business Profile accurate. Use the same name, address and phone number everywhere. List each surgeon's board certification by name.
  • Add a byline and a bio. Put the surgeon's name, credentials and a review date on medical content.
  • Use structured data that matches the page. FAQ, physician and organization markup helps machines read the page, as long as it reflects what a visitor can see.
  • Allow reputable crawlers on purpose. If you want AI answer tools to cite you, do not block them by accident. Our guide to ranking in AI Overviews explains what Google says about this.

What should you measure?

Traffic is not the goal. A consultation is. Track:

  • Consultation requests, by procedure and by source (Google, social, referral, email).
  • The share of requests that book, and the share of consultations that become surgery.
  • Clicks and impressions on each procedure page in Google Search Console.
  • Google Business Profile calls and direction requests.
  • A "how did you hear about us?" question on the form that includes AI tools such as ChatGPT.

Record a baseline for four to eight weeks before you change anything. We found no independent benchmark for how long plastic surgery content takes to produce consultations, and agency claims vary widely. Judge progress by impressions first, then clicks, then qualified inquiries.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Posting a photo without a signed authorization. This is the most common and the most costly.
  • Showing only your best cases. It builds distrust and may mislead.
  • Publishing thin procedure pages. A 300-word page cannot support the blog posts that link to it.
  • Writing as "The Team." Medical content needs a named, credentialed author.
  • Making promises. Words like "perfect," "guaranteed" and "no scars" create risk. Use ranges and state that results vary.
  • Copying competitors. If the page adds nothing new, a patient three months into research learns nothing from it.
  • Buying or faking reviews. It is against FTC rules, and AI-written reviews are covered too.
  • Letting content go stale. Add a review date and update recovery and risk pages when your protocols change.

A 30-day plan for one practice

  1. Week 1: Audit and rules. List your procedure pages and their word counts. Check that every photo and testimonial on your site has a signed authorization. Remove anything you cannot support.
  2. Week 2: Fix the top procedure page. Pick the procedure that matters most to revenue. Rewrite its page to cover candidacy, recovery, risks, results and cost factors, with the surgeon's byline and a review date.
  3. Week 3: Answer 10 questions. Collect the 10 most common consultation questions. Publish short answers on the page or as separate posts, and link them to the procedure page.
  4. Week 4: Add video and email. Record the surgeon's introduction and one question-and-answer clip. Set up the post-inquiry email. Start measuring.

Worked example (illustrative, not a client result)

Take a hypothetical solo practice that offers breast augmentation, a tummy tuck and a facelift. Its facelift page is 350 words and has no byline. The practice rewrites it to about 2,000 words with candidacy, recovery by week, risks and a comparison of mini and full facelifts. It adds the surgeon's credentials, a two-minute introduction video, a review date and an FAQ. It then publishes three recovery guides and links them to the page. It sets up a form question about how patients found the practice. No numbers are claimed here, because this example is a template. The point is the order: fix the core page, add questions and proof, then measure what patients do next.

Final thoughts

The best content marketing for plastic surgeons is honest, specific and compliant. Build the core procedure pages first, answer the questions patients really ask, show real results with permission, and measure consultations, not clicks. Do the privacy and advertising work up front. It protects patients, and it is part of what makes a practice trustworthy to both people and search engines.
