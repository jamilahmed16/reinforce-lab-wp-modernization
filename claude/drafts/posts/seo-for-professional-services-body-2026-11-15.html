SEO for professional services is the work of helping law firms, accountants, consultants, engineers, advisers and similar firms be found, and be trusted, when a buyer searches for expert help. The buyer is choosing people as much as a firm, so the pages that matter most are practice and service pages, partner profiles, proof of past work and clear ways to get in touch. Measure the work by qualified inquiries, not by traffic.

This guide covers how clients choose a professional firm, what the data shows about AI search, the keywords and pages that win, the rules you must respect, a 90-day plan and a worked example. We cite only sources we could read at the publisher, and we say when a figure is a vendor survey. This is marketing information, not legal or accounting advice. Check the advertising rules of your own professional body before you publish.

What is SEO for professional services?

It is search engine optimization for firms that sell expertise and judgment: law, accounting and tax, management and technology consulting, engineering, architecture, financial advice and similar fields. There is no product to demonstrate. Buyers look for evidence that specific people have handled a specific problem before. So the work combines normal SEO with proof of expertise, accurate profiles and a clear path to an inquiry.

How do clients choose a professional services firm?

Most engagements start with a trigger: a dispute, a deal, an audit, a new rule or a problem the buyer cannot solve alone. The buyer then builds a short list, often from a referral, and checks each name. A practitioner view from a UK agency is that, for specialist business advice, search often confirms a referral as much as it replaces one. That is an agency's observation, not a measured result, but it matches how most firms win work: people check you out before they call.

That checking is changing. Two 2026 surveys show where buyers now start.

  • VML's Future Buyer study. VML, a marketing agency, reports that in its professional services report, 58% of buyers begin supplier research on generative AI tools, ahead of traditional search engines (48%) and direct outreach (41%). It also reports that 34% form a firm supplier preference before ever speaking to one, and that 62% have shortlisted an unfamiliar supplier because of high-quality thought leadership. The series is based on first-party research with 750 buyers across eight international markets. It is an agency's study and we could not see the full method, so treat the figures as a direction.
  • Semrush's B2B buyer survey. Semrush surveyed 643 US B2B professionals in March and April 2026. Of the 519 who use AI at work, 66% regularly use it to research vendors and solutions, and 92% said AI had shaped their vendor shortlist. Agencies and service providers topped the list of things researched with AI, at 51%. About 14% of the respondents were in professional services. Semrush sells SEO software, and the sample is people who use AI at work.

For legal services, Clio's 2025 Legal Trends Report surveyed 1,000 US adults. It found that 24% had already used AI to answer a legal question, and 46% would consult AI before contacting a lawyer. Clio is a software company, and this is self-reported behavior.

The common message is that buyers now ask AI tools early, and that clear expertise helps you appear. It does not remove the need for classic SEO. It adds a second place to be found.

The pages that win clients

Practice and service pages

Give each practice, service and client type its own page. A single "Services" page with a paragraph for each topic rarely ranks for specific searches. Use the same outline for each page so partners can review it quickly.

SectionWhat it answers
Who it is forThe industry, size or situation, in the first screen
The problemWhat the client faces, in their words
The processSteps, timeline and who they will work with
ProofCredentials, representative work (anonymized if required), publications
FeesHow you charge and what changes the price
Next stepOne clear action: a call, a consultation or a proposal request

Partner and team profiles

Buyers evaluate people. A profile should show roles, credentials, areas of focus, the kind of matters handled, published work, talks and a direct way to get in touch. Thin profiles with only a title and a school lose referred clients at the last step.

Industry pages

If you serve sectors such as healthcare, manufacturing or software, give each its own page with real examples and the rules that matter in that sector.

Insight articles and guides

Write from your own work. Short partner interviews are a good source of content that carries your firm's view. Answer the questions clients ask on calls, put the answer first, cite sources and date the page. Google warns against making many pages mainly to manipulate results, so build fewer, better pages.

Case studies

Proof matters, and confidentiality limits it. Use anonymized examples, or get written consent to name a client. State the situation, what you did, the result and the dates. Never overstate a result, and follow your professional body's rules on results and testimonials.

Location pages

Build a location page only for an office or a market where you actually work, and make it useful, with local details, who is based there and how to visit. Pages that only swap a city name add risk and no value.

What proof do buyers trust?

Buyers of expert services cannot test the work first, so they look for signs that it will be done well. The strongest signs are those that a stranger can check.

  • Credentials they can verify. A license number, a registry entry or a professional body listing, linked from the bio.
  • Named people with a track record. Experience in the buyer's sector, with dates and the type of work.
  • Published thinking. Articles, talks, research and rule-change explainers that show how you reason. VML's survey suggests this thought leadership can put a firm on a short list. Treat that as a survey result from an agency, and still make the content real and useful.
  • Reviews from real clients. On Google and the sites your field uses, collected within your body's rules.
  • Clear fees and process. Buyers worry about cost and effort. A page that explains both builds trust.
  • Consistent facts. The same names, offices and services across your site, directories and social profiles.

How do business buyers differ from private clients?

Private clients, such as a family with a will or a person with a tax return, search by service and place, compare prices and decide quickly. Local SEO, reviews and clear fees matter most. Business buyers, such as a general counsel or a finance director, search rarely, read deeply and ask colleagues. Partner profiles, sector pages and published views matter most, and a referral often starts the process. Many firms serve both, so build the pages for each group separately, with their own questions, tone and calls to action.

What rules do you need to respect?

Professional bodies set advertising and conduct rules, and they differ by field and place. For lawyers in the US, ABA Model Rule 7.1 says a lawyer shall not make a false or misleading communication about the lawyer or the lawyer's services, and states adopt their own versions. Accountants, engineers, financial advisers and others have their own bodies and rules. Common themes are the same:

  • No guarantee or implied promise of a result.
  • No unsupported claims such as "the best" or "leading".
  • Client confidentiality, and consent before you name a client or use a logo.
  • Accurate use of titles, credentials and specialist labels.
  • Rules on testimonials, reviews and how you ask for them.
  • A named person who approves each page.

Write the rules for each credential and place down, and decide who signs off each page. Our guide to generative engine optimization notes that anything on your site can be quoted by an AI tool, so every claim must be accurate and current.

Local SEO for offices

For firms with offices, local search combines the office and the service. Each office needs its own Google Business Profile with the right primary category and honest secondary categories, the same name, address and phone number on your site and every directory, and reviews that you earn from real clients and answer politely. Reviews that name the service help more than "great team". Follow your professional body's rules on how you ask for reviews. Our local SEO service covers this base.

How do you show up in AI search?

The steps are the same ones that serve SEO, with a few additions. Make sure search crawlers can reach your pages. Keep the facts about your firm, partners and services the same across your site and profiles. Put a direct answer at the top of each practice page, with named sources. Earn honest mentions on the sites that AI answers cite, such as directories, professional bodies, press and review sites. Then test with a fixed list of buyer questions, run three times in several AI tools. Our guides to checking if AI tools mention your brand and ranking in AI Overviews explain how.

One caution. Google lists no extra technical requirements for AI Overviews beyond being indexed and eligible to show with a snippet. So do not buy special markup or files in the hope of a shortcut.

How do you measure it?

Measure by practice, not as one "leads" number. Track consultation requests and proposal requests from organic landing pages, the share that become real clients, impressions and clicks for each practice page, Google Business Profile calls and direction requests, and branded search. Add a question to your intake form: "How did you hear about us?", with an option for AI tools. Use Google's Generative AI performance report in Search Console for AI impressions, and a prompt sheet for mentions. If a practice page ranks and no one inquires, the offer or the form is the problem. If nothing ranks, the page depth and keyword map are the problem.

Worked example: a 30-person accounting firm

This is an illustration, not a client result. Imagine a firm with two offices that offers audit, tax and advisory services. Its site has one "Services" page and short partner bios. Its two office listings use different phone numbers.

The team lists the matters it wants more of: tax planning for owner-managed businesses, and audit for nonprofits. It builds two practice pages with the outline above, with a fees section and a named reviewer. It rewrites four partner profiles with credentials, sectors and published work. It fixes the phone numbers, and asks clients for honest reviews in line with its professional body's rules. It publishes a partner-led guide on year-end tax steps, with dated sources. It also tests 15 buyer questions three times in three AI tools and records the result.

After 90 days it compares inquiries for the two practices with the baseline and repeats its prompt test. The result may be small or large. What matters is that the plan was built around real matters and measured by inquiries.

Should you hire an SEO agency for a professional services firm?

Hire help when you lack time, need technical fixes or want a plan before you spend on content. Choose one that understands your professional body's rules and says so in writing. Ask for a keyword and page map by practice, a list of technical fixes, a sample report tied to inquiries and a statement of who approves each page. What are the common mistakes?

  • One page for every service. Specific searches need specific pages.
  • Thin partner profiles. Buyers judge the people.
  • Generic content. Articles written without partner input read like everyone else's.
  • Ignoring the rules. A claim that breaches your body's rules is a risk, however well it ranks.
  • Naming clients without consent. Confidentiality comes first.
  • Measuring only traffic. Track inquiries by practice.
  • Forgetting AI search. Buyers now ask AI tools early.
