To write SEO friendly blog posts, pick one topic that a real person searches for, check what already ranks and what it misses, then write a clear, original answer with a unique title, short sections, proof, useful links and a plain description. Put the answer first, name the author, cite sources you have read, and update the post when the facts change. Search engines reward content made for people, so the best SEO step is to be more useful than what is already there.

This guide gives you 12 steps in the order we use them, the data on why most posts get no search traffic, a checklist you can copy, and a worked example. It replaces our older post on the same topic. We cite only sources we could read at the publisher, and we say where a figure is a vendor's own study.

What is an SEO friendly blog post?

It is a post that is useful to a reader and easy for a search engine to find, read and understand. The two goals fit together. Google says SEO can be a helpful activity when it is applied to people-first content, but not when the content is made mainly to attract search visits.

An SEO friendly post has six traits:

  • One clear topic and one primary keyword that match a real search need.
  • A unique, accurate title and a direct answer near the top.
  • Original value: first-hand experience, examples or data that other pages lack.
  • Clear headings, short sentences and easy-to-scan sections.
  • Honest signals of trust: an author, sources and a date that means something.
  • Good links, useful images and a page that loads fast on a phone.

Why do most blog posts get no search traffic?

Ahrefs, an SEO software vendor, studied about 14 billion pages in its own index. It found that 96.55% of pages get no traffic from Google, and 1.94% get one to ten visits a month. Ahrefs notes that the sample leans toward the quality side of the web and that its traffic numbers are estimates, so the real share may differ. Its newer statistics roundup says only 1.74% of newly published pages reach the top 10 for at least one keyword within a year, and that 72.9% of pages in the top 10 are more than three years old.

These figures do not mean blogging is pointless. They mean that publishing alone is not enough. Posts that earn traffic usually match what people want, add something new, and get links and time. That is the aim of the steps below.

12 steps to write SEO friendly blog posts

Step 1: Choose a topic with real value

Start with your customers, not a keyword list. Ask: Is this a question my audience has? Can I add something that others have not? If I rank, will it help my business? Then use keyword research to check demand and to choose the words people use. Pick one primary keyword for each post and two or three related phrases or questions. A tool such as Semrush or Ahrefs helps, and free sources such as Google Search Console, autocomplete and "People also ask" are a good start.

Step 2: Check search intent and study the top results

Search your keyword and read the top 10 pages on the search engine results page (SERP). Note what kind of page ranks: a how-to, a list, a comparison, a tool or a definition. Note what each page covers and what it leaves out. Your post must fit the intent. If everyone ranking is a step-by-step guide, a long opinion piece will struggle. Then plan what you can add: a fresher statistic, a worked example, a table, an expert view or a question the others skip. We run this check on every post before we write.

Step 3: Write a unique, accurate title

Google's guidance says a good title is unique to the page, clear and concise, and accurately describes the content. Put the primary keyword near the front and aim for about 60 characters so it is less likely to be cut off. Avoid exaggeration. Google's quality questions ask whether the main heading avoids being shocking or overstated. Do not expect your title to show exactly as written. Ahrefs found that Google rewrote title tags 33.4% of the time in its study, and that it was more likely to rewrite titles that were too long. When Google ignored the title tag, it used the H1 about half the time, so make your H1 and title agree.

Step 4: Start with the answer

Give the main answer in the first two or three sentences, then explain. Readers get what they need fast. Search engines and AI answer tools can also pull a clean answer from the page. Semrush recommends starting each section with a direct answer for the same reason. Skip long warm-ups that say nothing new.

Step 5: Outline with one H1 and clear H2 and H3 headings

Use one H1 for the title. Use H2 headings for the main sections and H3 headings for the parts within them. Write headings that say what the section answers, and where it fits, use a question that people ask, such as "How long should a blog post be?" This helps people scan, and it shows search engines how the page is organized. Do not skip levels or use headings only for style.

Step 6: Add original value

This step matters most. Google asks whether content provides original information, reporting, research or analysis, and whether it adds substantial value when compared with other pages in the results. If you mainly summarize others, you add little. Add what only you can: results from your own work, photos you took, a process you follow, a mistake you made, a price you quote, or a clear opinion with reasons. Add a table, a checklist or a worked example when it helps. Do not write to a target word count. Google says it has no preferred word count. Write as long as the topic needs.

Step 7: Write so people can read it

  • Use short sentences and short paragraphs of two to three sentences.
  • Choose everyday words, and explain a term the first time you use it.
  • Use bullet points for lists and numbered steps for procedures.
  • Use active voice. Say who does what.
  • Read the draft out loud, then cut anything that does not help.

A note on scores: tools that grade reading level can help you write plainly, but Ahrefs found no correlation between Flesch Reading Ease and ranking position. Use them to help readers, not to chase a ranking. We aim for grade 8 or lower because most people read faster that way.

Step 8: Show who wrote it and why you can be trusted

Google's "Who, How and Why" test is a good checklist. It ties to what Google calls E-E-A-T: experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust, with trust the most important. Who wrote this? Put a byline that links to a short bio with real credentials. How was it made? Say so if you used automation or AI in a major way. Why was it made? The answer should be to help people. Cite the sources you used and link to them. Only quote a statistic that you have read at the source, and say how old it is and what its limits are. Do not fake an author, use made-up credentials or hide how the post was made.

Step 9: Write a plain meta description

The meta description is a short summary that may appear under your title in search results. Google says a good one is short, unique to the page and includes the most relevant points. Keep it to about 140 to 160 characters, include the keyword once and say what the reader will get. Do not expect it to show every time. Ahrefs found that Google showed the meta description in results only 37.22% of the time and rewrote it 62.78% of the time, and that 25.02% of top-ranking pages had none. Write one anyway. It costs little, and it is your best shot at controlling the snippet.

Step 10: Use a short, clear URL

Use a slug that names the topic in a few words, such as /how-to-write-seo-friendly-blog-posts/. Use lowercase letters and hyphens. Leave out dates, extra words and IDs. Once a URL is live and earning traffic, do not change it without a redirect plan.

Step 11: Add useful links

  • Internal links: link to related posts and to the page that serves the reader's next step. Google says every page you care about should have a link from at least one other page on your site, and that there is no magic number of links. Use descriptive link text, also called anchor text, not "click here."
  • External links: link to the primary source for each statistic or claim. Link only to pages you trust. If you do not trust a page but still want to link to it, add a nofollow or similar attribute.

Step 12: Add images with alt text

Use sharp, relevant images near the text they relate to. Add short, descriptive alt text that explains what the image shows and how it relates to the page. Leave out stuffed keywords. Compress files so the page loads quickly on a phone. Add a featured image that fits the topic, and keep each image's file name plain, such as seo-checklist-table.png.

How do you make a post useful for AI answers too?

The same habits help AI answer tools, since they pull clear, well-sourced passages. Lead each section with a direct answer, keep one idea per paragraph, use headings that match real questions, name the author, cite primary sources and show the date of the last real update. Semrush cites an AirOps study that found fresh content was one of the strongest predictors of AI visibility. That is a vendor-cited study, so treat it as a signal, not a rule. Our guide to ranking in AI Overviews goes deeper.

Should you use AI to write blog posts?

You can, with care. Google says appropriate use of AI is not against its guidelines. Using AI mainly to manipulate rankings is. Its guidance on scaled content abuse says that generating many pages without adding value for users may break its spam policy. Google also says generative models predict likely words and do not retrieve facts, so you must fact-check everything, including titles, meta descriptions and alt text. A safe approach:

  • Use AI for outlines, rewording and first drafts, not as the final author.
  • Add your own experience, examples, numbers and photos.
  • Check every fact and statistic at the source.
  • Have a named person review and own the post.
  • Do not publish dozens of near-identical pages.

How often should you update a post?

Update a post when its facts change, when a better source appears, or when it starts to lose traffic. A real update means new data, new steps or a corrected claim, and a date that reflects it. Google's guidance lists changing the date of a page to make it look fresh, when the content has not substantially changed, as a warning sign. It also says that adding or removing a lot of content just to seem fresh will not help. Show the last-updated date only when you earned it.

A 90-minute workflow

  1. 15 minutes: choose the keyword, read the top 10 and list what is missing.
  2. 10 minutes: write the title, the answer and an outline.
  3. 40 minutes: write the draft with your own examples and sources.
  4. 15 minutes: edit for short sentences, add links and images, and write the meta description.
  5. 10 minutes: run the checklist, preview on a phone and publish.

After publishing, check Google Search Console in a few weeks. Look at impressions, clicks and the queries that bring people. Improve the post based on what you see.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Writing for the keyword, not the reader. Stuffing a phrase into every line hurts readability and trust.
  • Copying the top results. If you add nothing new, there is no reason to rank above them.
  • Publishing thin posts. Short, vague pages rarely earn traffic.
  • Skipping the byline and sources. They are quick trust signals.
  • Faking freshness. Changing the date without changing the content can backfire.
  • Ignoring mobile. A slow, cramped page loses readers.
  • Not measuring. Without data, you cannot improve.

Worked example (illustrative, not a client result)

Take a hypothetical accounting firm that wants to rank for "how to track small business expenses." It reads the top 10 results and finds mostly generic lists with no examples. It chooses a how-to format, adds a downloadable sheet, a worked example with sample numbers it can support, and a short section on mistakes it sees with new clients. The title is "How to Track Small Business Expenses: A Simple 5-Step Method." The post opens with the answer, names the partner who wrote it, links to the tax authority's rules and to two of the firm's own pages, and adds a clear call to book a review. No traffic numbers are claimed, because this is a template. The point is the process: study the results, add original value, then build the page cleanly.

Final thoughts

SEO friendly blog posts are not a formula. They are useful posts with clean signals. Choose a topic worth your reader's time, check what already ranks, add something new, and make the page clear and trustworthy. Measure what happens, and keep the post true as things change.
