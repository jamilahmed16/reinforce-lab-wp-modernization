GEO and SEO are two ways to be found, and they overlap more than most articles admit. SEO aims to rank your pages in a list of search results. GEO, or generative engine optimization, aims to get your brand named or cited inside an answer that an AI tool writes. Most of the work that makes a page rank also makes it easier for an AI tool to quote. What changes is where you appear, what you measure and how much of your visibility depends on other websites.

This guide compares the two in plain terms. It shows what changes, what stays the same, what the data says about ranking and AI citations, and how to split your time. It includes two worked examples, a decision table, a measurement plan and a 90-day plan. If you need the basics first, read our guide to what generative engine optimization is.

What is the difference between GEO and SEO?

SEO is the practice of improving your site so it ranks in search engines such as Google and Bing and earns clicks. GEO is the practice of improving your content and brand so AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google AI Mode mention or cite you in their written answers. SEO competes for a position in a list. GEO competes for a place in the paragraph.

That table is a simplification. It matches the comparisons published by Ahrefs and Semrush, and it matches what Google says about its own AI features. The last row is the important one. The foundation is shared.

What stays the same?

A lot stays the same. Google states that the technical requirements for appearing in its AI Overviews and AI Mode are the same as for Google Search. A page must be indexed and eligible to show with a snippet. There are no extra technical requirements. Google also says that the SEO basics still apply: allow crawling, link pages together, give people a good page experience, keep important content in text and keep structured data matched to the visible page.

These parts do not change:

  • Access. If your page is blocked or not indexed, neither a ranking nor an AI citation is likely.
  • Useful content. A page that answers a real question well helps in both.
  • Search intent. People still want a clear answer to a clear need, whether they type it into a search box or ask it in a chat.
  • Trust. Real authors, honest facts and a reputation outside your own site help in both.
  • A working site. Slow pages, broken pages and content hidden behind scripts make both jobs harder.

This is why we do not sell GEO as a replacement for SEO. If your site has crawl, indexing or content problems, fix those first. Our search engine optimization service starts there for the same reason.

What changes?

Five things change in practice.

1. The result is a mention, not a position

In SEO you can see "position 3". In an AI answer there is no position 3. You are named, cited or missing. A link may appear as a small icon, a side panel or a card at the bottom of the answer.

2. The journey may end without a click

The Pew Research Center studied the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025. When a Google search page showed an AI summary, people clicked a regular result link in 8% of visits. When there was no summary, they clicked in 15% of visits. They clicked a link inside the AI summary in only 1% of visits. They also ended their browsing session more often on pages with a summary: 26% of the time, against 16%.

These figures describe Google AI Overviews in one month for one group of US adults. They do not describe ChatGPT or other tools, and they do not describe your site. They do show why a mention can matter even when it sends no click.

3. The scoreboard is different

For SEO you track rankings, clicks and conversions. For GEO you track how often your brand is named and cited, which pages are cited and who else appears. Because answers vary from run to run, you read the trend, not a single result.

4. Other sites matter more

AI tools often draw on third-party pages: reviews, "best of" lists, news and forums. Ahrefs reported that branded web mentions showed the strongest link with brand visibility in AI Overviews, with a correlation of 0.664 in its data. A correlation is not proof that mentions cause visibility, but it points to why PR and reviews matter more in GEO than in classic SEO.

5. Questions are longer

Pew found that only 8% of one or two word searches produced an AI summary, while 53% of searches with ten words or more did. Searches that began with a question word such as who, what, when or why produced one 60% of the time. People use AI tools for longer, more detailed questions, so pages that answer specific questions have an edge.

Do you still need to rank in Google to be cited by AI?

Ranking helps, but it is no longer enough on its own, and the numbers have moved quickly. Here is what we could verify from the sources themselves.

  • July 2025: Ahrefs found that about 76% of pages cited in Google AI Overviews also ranked in the top 10 for the same query. That study looked at 1.9 million citations.
  • March 2026: Ahrefs repeated the study with 863,000 keyword results and 4 million AI Overview URLs. About 38% of cited URLs ranked in the top 10 when it counted every block on the page. When it counted blue links only, 37.1% were in the top 10, 26.2% ranked 11 to 100 and 36.7% did not rank in the top 100.
  • AI assistants: In an earlier Ahrefs study, an average of about 12% of citations from AI assistants also ranked in Google's top 10 for the original prompt. Perplexity was the outlier, with nearly one in three.

Ahrefs suggests that fan-out explains part of the drop. The tool splits a question into related searches and cites pages that show up for those sub-questions, even when they do not rank for the original query. Google confirms that AI Overviews and AI Mode may use query fan-out. Treat Ahrefs' explanation as an informed guess, because Google does not publish the fan-out queries.

You may see other numbers online, such as claims that overlap fell below 20%. We only quote studies we read at the source. Numbers differ by method, tool and date, so avoid building a plan on one percentage.

The practical reading is simple. Ranking for your main keyword gives you a better chance, but a page that ranks nowhere can still be cited, and a page that ranks first can be skipped. This is why topic coverage matters. A set of pages that answer the related questions gives fan-out more places to find you.

Is GEO the same as AEO and LLM optimization?

The names overlap, and people use them in different ways. Answer engine optimization (AEO) usually means writing short, direct answers that can be shown as a featured snippet, a voice answer or an AI summary. LLM optimization and "AI SEO" usually mean the same goal as GEO: being used by large language models when they write answers. Ahrefs describes GEO as also known as AEO or LLMO.

Google makes a point about these labels. Its guidance says that "AEO" and "GEO" are terms used for work aimed at AI search, and that from its view, optimizing for generative AI search is still SEO. It also advises caution with third-party "AEO" or "GEO" advice and services. That is fair advice. Ask any provider what exactly they will change, how they will measure it and which of their claims you can check yourself.

For your own planning, you do not need to settle the naming. Pick the work that fits your buyers: clear answers, reliable facts, strong pages, real mentions elsewhere and honest measurement.

How should the content differ for SEO and GEO?

Good content for one is mostly good content for the other. A few habits matter more when you want an AI tool to quote you.

  • Lead with the answer. Put a direct answer in the first one or two sentences under each question heading. A tool can lift that piece without the rest of the page.
  • Use specific facts. Add numbers, dates and named sources. A claim with a source and a year is easier to trust than a general statement.
  • Cover related questions. A cluster of pages that answer the follow-up questions gives fan-out more places to find you. Write each page because a reader needs it, not to catch every wording.
  • Show who wrote it. Use a real author, a short bio and a clear update date.
  • Add what only you know. Google asks for content with a unique point of view and first-hand experience. Your own data, your own process and your own results are the parts nobody else can copy.

None of these habits hurt your rankings. That is the main reason to run both together.

Should you focus on SEO or GEO?

For most businesses the answer is SEO first, then GEO on top. SEO is the established channel and the base that AI tools draw on. GEO adds habits for AI answers and a new way to measure. The balance depends on how your buyers search.

Should you hire separate teams? In most cases no. The work shares one technical and editorial base, so splitting it can create two sets of pages that compete. You do need a GEO measurement method on top of your normal SEO tools.

Worked example 1: a page that ranks but is not cited

This is an illustration, not a client result. Imagine a company that sells payroll software for small shops. Its page "payroll software for small business" ranks fourth on Google. The team tests 20 buyer questions in four AI tools. The brand is named in 3 of 80 answers, and the ranking page is cited in none.

They compare their page with the pages that are cited. The cited pages have a short answer in the first lines, a clear price range with a date and a named source for tax rules. They also see that two review sites and a news article are cited often, and that none of them mention the company.

The fixes are plain. The team adds a short answer at the top of the page, adds the price range with an update date, and links the tax authority's guidance as a source. They write two new pages for the related questions that came up: "how to run payroll for part-time staff" and "what payroll records to keep". They also ask the two review sites to review the product.

Their SEO work stays in place. They do not rewrite the page for AI tools. They watch the trend in monthly tests, and they check Search Console for the ranking page. If the ranking falls after the changes, they roll the edit back, because a lost ranking costs more than a missed citation.

Worked example 2: a local service where SEO leads

Again, this is an illustration. Imagine a plumbing company that serves one city. Most of its leads come from searches such as "emergency plumber near me" and from the map pack. The owner asks whether to spend the budget on GEO.

The team tests 15 questions in AI tools. For emergency searches, the tools often show a map list or name a few firms from directories. For general advice questions such as "why is my water heater leaking", the tools quote guides from larger sites, and the company cannot win those.

The plan puts most of the work into local SEO. It checks the Google Business Profile, fixes name, address and phone details across directories, and gathers real reviews. A small share goes to GEO. The owner writes one clear page on what to do when a water heater leaks, with real steps and a note on when to call a plumber, and signs it with a short bio.

The lesson is to match the effort to the channel where buyers act. For a local emergency service, rankings and maps drive most of the calls, so SEO leads and GEO plays a smaller part.

How do you measure both?

Run one report with two sections.

SEO section

  • Rankings for your target terms
  • Organic clicks and conversions from Search Console and analytics
  • Indexed pages and technical health

GEO section

  • Prompt tests. Write 15 to 25 real buyer questions. Run them each month in the same AI tools. Record whether you are named, whether you are linked, which pages are cited and which competitors appear.
  • Mention share. The share of answers that name your brand.
  • Citation share. The share of answers that cite your domain.
  • AI referral visits. In analytics, filter visits from AI tool domains such as chatgpt.com or perplexity.ai. Check your own data to see which domains actually appear.
  • Brand accuracy. Note when an answer gets a fact about you wrong and trace the source.

Be careful with early results. Ahrefs reported that AI search visitors on its own site were a tiny share of traffic yet a large share of signups. That is one company. Another study it cites found that not every site sees that lift. Measure your own site before you assume AI visitors convert better.

There is also no public "prompt volume" for AI tools. Search Engine Land notes that ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity do not share what users ask, so you cannot size a topic the way you can with keyword tools. Use your sales calls and support emails to find the real questions.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Treating GEO as a replacement. SEO is the base. Dropping it can remove your chance of being retrieved at all.
  • Using one study as proof. Citation overlap numbers changed sharply within a year. Plan on principles, not one percentage.
  • Splitting the work in two. Separate teams can publish pages that compete with each other.
  • Chasing special files. Google says it does not use llms.txt files and that special markup is not needed. Fix pages and facts first.
  • Ignoring other sites. If AI tools cite review sites and lists, your own pages are only part of the picture.
  • Testing once. AI answers change. One test tells you little.
  • Writing for tools, not people. Google asks for content with a real point of view and first-hand experience. Pages built only to catch every variation of a question can break its spam policy.

A 90-day plan to run both

Days 1 to 30: base and baseline

Check crawling and indexing. Fix technical problems. Write your list of buyer questions. Run the first prompt tests and record rankings, clicks and AI mentions side by side.

Days 31 to 60: improve your best pages

Pick your five most important pages. Add a direct answer, named sources and an honest update date. Write one or two pages for questions where AI tools cite others and not you. Check that your company facts match across your site, profiles and listings.

Days 61 to 90: earn mentions and review

Find the review sites, lists and news pages that AI tools already cite for your topic. Pitch one or two where you have a real story or real data. Run the tests again, compare with the baseline and decide what to repeat. Keep the pace steady, with a few pages a month rather than a burst.
