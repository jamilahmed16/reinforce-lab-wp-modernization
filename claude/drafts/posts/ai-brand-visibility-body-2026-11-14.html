AI brand visibility is how often AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google AI Mode name, describe or link to your brand when people ask questions about your market. You can get a first reading in about 30 minutes with a spreadsheet and three browser tabs. This guide gives you the test, the scoring, two worked examples and a plan to repeat it each month.

It also tells you what a 30-minute test cannot do. AI answers change from run to run, so one check is a sample, not a verdict. A large study by SparkToro found that AI tools almost never give the same list of brands twice. So we show you how to read a first test fairly and how to build a trend over time.

What is AI brand visibility?

AI brand visibility is a set of separate questions, not one number. A tool may name you but not link to you. It may link to you but describe you wrongly. It may name your competitors and leave you out. Measure each question on its own:

  • Mention rate: in what share of answers is your brand named?
  • Citation rate: in what share of answers does the tool link to a page of yours?
  • Share of voice: of all the brands named across your test, what share is yours?
  • Accuracy: when you are named, is the description right?
  • Sources: which pages does the tool cite, and are they yours or someone else's?

Ahrefs lists similar measures in its AI visibility audit guide: mentions, citations, share of voice, and a check of accuracy, tone and whether you are the lead recommendation. Some tools combine these into one "AI visibility score". Treat a combined score with care until you know what goes into it.

Why does one test not tell the full story?

AI tools write each answer fresh. The same question can give different brands on different runs, in different orders. SparkToro and Gumshoe tested this in a study published in January 2026. About 600 volunteers ran 12 prompts through ChatGPT, Claude and Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode, for nearly 3,000 runs in all. The main findings were:

  • There was less than a 1 in 100 chance that ChatGPT or Google's AI would give the same list of brands in any two responses to the same prompt.
  • There was less than a 1 in 1,000 chance of seeing the same list in the same order.
  • Even so, some brands came up again and again across dozens of runs. Appearing in most runs of a prompt showed a brand the tool tended to link with that question.

The authors draw two lessons. First, a "position in AI answers" figure is not meaningful, since the order is close to random. Second, the share of runs in which you appear is a reasonable measure, if you run each prompt many times. Our 30-minute test gives you a baseline sample. To turn it into a real rate, you repeat it, which we cover below.

Google makes a related point for its own tools. It says no third-party tool has access to its internal ranking or AI systems, and it tells site owners to use the Generative AI performance report in Search Console to see how their content does in its AI features.

How do you run the 30-minute test?

You need a spreadsheet, a notepad and the ability to open ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity. If you can only test two tools, test the two your buyers use most. Here is the time budget.

StepTimeWhat you do
1. Write the prompts5 minutesWrite 10 buyer questions in three groups
2. Run them15 minutesPaste each into three tools and copy the answers
3. Log the results5 minutesFill six columns for each answer
4. Score and read5 minutesWork out four numbers and note the gaps

Step 1: Write 10 prompts (5 minutes)

Use three groups. The mix matters, because a tool may know you well when asked by name and still leave you out of a category question.

  • Category prompts (4): questions where no brand is named. Example: "What is the best invoicing software for a small accounting firm?"
  • Comparison prompts (3): you against a named competitor, or two competitors. Example: "How does [your brand] compare with [competitor]?"
  • Branded prompts (3): questions about you. Example: "What does [your brand] do, and who is it for?"

Take the wording from real sources: sales calls, support emails, the "People also ask" box and the questions prospects send you. Write the way a buyer writes, not the way your product page reads. Keep the prompts fixed once you pick them, because changing them makes later comparisons useless.

Step 2: Run them in three tools (15 minutes)

Open each tool in a fresh session. Where you can, turn off memory and personalization, or use a private window and log out, so your own history does not shape the answer. Paste each prompt, wait for the full answer and copy it into your sheet. Use the web search mode if the tool offers one, since that is how many buyers use it.

For Google, run the same question in a logged-out window and check whether an AI Overview appears. Not every question triggers one. Pew Research found in March 2025 that about 18% of Google searches in its study produced an AI summary, and that longer and question-style searches produced them more often. If no overview appears, write "none" and move on.

Step 3: Log six columns (5 minutes)

For each answer, record:

  1. Named: yes or no.
  2. Linked: does the answer link to a page on your domain?
  3. Position: first, middle, last or not applicable. Treat this as a rough note only, given the random order.
  4. Competitors named: list them.
  5. Accuracy: right, partly wrong or wrong, checked against your own facts.
  6. Cited pages: copy every URL the tool shows, yours or not.

If you have time, add a seventh note: the tone. Mark each mention positive, neutral or negative. "The best option for beginners" and "the expensive option" are both mentions, but they do very different work for you. Ahrefs lists sentiment and framing, differentiation and whether you are the lead recommendation as things to check in an AI visibility audit.

Step 4: Score it (5 minutes)

Work out four numbers.

  • Mention rate = answers that name you, divided by all answers.
  • Citation rate = answers that link to your domain, divided by all answers.
  • Share of voice = your mentions, divided by all brand mentions across every answer.
  • Accuracy rate = answers about you that are right, divided by answers that name you.

Then list the cited pages that are not yours. That list shows where the tools get their facts about your market.

Worked example 1: an HR software company

This is an illustration, not a client result. Imagine a company that sells HR software to firms with 50 to 200 staff. It runs 10 prompts in three tools, so 30 answers in all.

  • It is named in 6 of the 30 answers. Mention rate: 6 divided by 30, which is 20%.
  • A page on its own domain is linked in 2 answers. Citation rate: about 7%.
  • Across all 30 answers, the tools name 84 brands in total, and 6 of those mentions are the company. Share of voice: 6 divided by 84, about 7%.
  • Of the 6 answers that name it, 4 describe it correctly. Accuracy rate: about 67%.

The breakdown by prompt type tells more than the totals. The company is named in all 3 branded answers in one tool but in only 1 of the 12 category answers. The wrong descriptions all repeat one error: they say the company serves "enterprises only", based on a review page from three years ago.

The fixes follow from the data. The team corrects the old review page by contacting the site. It updates its own "who we serve" page with a clear first sentence and a date. It writes one page that answers the category question "what HR software suits a firm with 100 staff", with real pricing ranges and a named source for the legal points. It then repeats the test a month later with the same 10 prompts.

Worked example 2: a cybersecurity consultancy

This is another illustration. Imagine a consultancy that sells security reviews to mid-sized companies. It runs the same test: 30 answers.

  • It is named in 14 of 30 answers. Mention rate: about 47%.
  • It is linked in 3 answers. Citation rate: 10%.
  • All 9 branded answers name it. Only 5 of the 21 other answers do.

At first the 47% looks healthy. The split shows the real position. The tools know the brand when asked by name, but they rarely recommend it for category and comparison questions. The cited pages for those questions are two review sites and a trade association list, and the consultancy is on none of them.

The plan is not to rewrite its own pages. It is to earn a place where the tools already look. The team checks which review sites list its competitors, applies to be listed, and offers the trade body a short, original guide with data from its own reviews. It also adds a direct answer to its main service page.

The lesson is to split your results. A good total can hide a gap in the questions that matter most for new buyers.

Check access early. Ahrefs notes that some content delivery networks block AI crawlers by default, so a site can be hidden from some tools without anyone deciding it. Make sure robots.txt, your firewall and your CDN allow the crawlers you want. For Google, a page must be indexed and eligible to show with a snippet to appear as a supporting link in its AI features.

How do you turn the test into a trend?

Repeat it. A baseline plus four monthly rounds with the same prompts gives you a rate and a range, which is far more useful than one number.

  1. Keep the prompts fixed. Add new ones in a second list, so the trend stays clean.
  2. Use the same tools and conditions. Same fresh-session method, same country, same month day.
  3. Run important prompts more than once. If you can spare time, run each prompt three times per tool. If you appear in one of three, that is inconsistent visibility, which is different from appearing in all three.
  4. Record the average and the range. Report "named in 3 to 5 of 10 runs", not "named".
  5. Add competitors. Track three to five, since share of voice is a ratio.
  6. Note what changed. A new page, a press story or a model update can explain a shift.

When your list grows past 30 to 40 prompts or covers several markets, a paid tool saves time, because it can run many prompts often. Be careful with any tool that sells a "ranking position in AI answers". Given the order findings above, that figure has little meaning. Look for tools that report the share of runs in which you appear, show the full answers and name the sources.

What should you do after the test?

Work from the gaps, not from a generic list.

  1. Fix wrong facts first. A wrong answer about your price, service or location costs more than a missed mention. Update your page and the cited third-party page.
  2. Add direct answers. Put a clear answer in the first sentences of your key pages and name your sources.
  3. Make brand facts consistent. Match your name, services, location and leaders across your site, profiles and listings.
  4. Close content gaps. Where the tools answer a buyer question and cite others, write one useful page.
  5. Earn mentions where tools already look. Use the cited-page list to plan outreach with a real story or real data.
  6. Check traffic. In analytics, look for visits from AI tool domains such as chatgpt.com or perplexity.ai. Check your own data to see which domains appear.

Why does my brand show up in one tool and not another?

Each tool builds its answers from its own sources and methods, so a brand can be visible in one and missing in another on the same day. Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode draw on Google's search index, and Google says a page must be indexed and eligible to show with a snippet to be a supporting link. Other tools use their own crawls and partner indexes.

Ahrefs has measured the difference. In its study, on average only about 12% of the citations from AI assistants also ranked in Google's top 10 for the original prompt, and Perplexity was the outlier at nearly one in three. Ahrefs also found that the most cited brands differ between Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT and Perplexity.

So do not blend the tools into one number too early. Score each tool on its own first, then compare. A pattern such as strong in one tool and weak in two others points to a source problem. You may be present on the pages that one tool trusts and absent from the pages the others use. The cited-page list from step 3 shows which pages those are.

What is a good AI share of voice?

There is no reliable public benchmark. Share of voice depends on how many brands the tools name for your prompts, and that number differs a lot between markets. In a market where the tools name three brands per answer, a share near 33% is average. In a market where they name 15, a share near 7% is average. So compare yourself with the competitors in your own test, and with your own baseline from last month.

A few rules keep the number honest:

  • Use the same prompts and tools each time.
  • Count a brand once per answer, however many times the answer repeats its name.
  • Track three to five named competitors, so you can see who gains when you lose.
  • Look at the number by prompt type. Your share on category questions matters most for new buyers.

Be wary of any article that gives a "good" percentage without saying how it was measured. Without the prompt set, the tools and the market, a benchmark means little.

Can analytics track AI mentions?

Only in part. Analytics tools such as Google Analytics can show visits that arrive from an AI tool, if the tool sends a referrer. They cannot show a mention that sent no click. Pew Research found that people clicked a link inside a Google AI summary in only 1% of visits in its March 2025 data, so many mentions will never appear in your reports.

That is why the prompt test matters. It measures the part analytics cannot see. Use both together:

  • Prompt tests show whether you are named, linked and described correctly.
  • Referral visits show how many people clicked through, and what they did next.
  • Branded search and direct visits may rise when mentions build awareness, but they have other causes, so treat a rise as a clue and not as proof.
  • A lead source question on your forms, such as "how did you hear about us", can catch buyers who say they used an AI tool.

Search Console is part of this too. Google counts traffic from its AI features in the normal Search Console reports and also offers a Generative AI performance report. Use it for Google's tools. For ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity, the prompt test and your referral data are what you have.

Which tools should you test, and are free checkers worth using?

Start with the tools your buyers use. For most businesses that means ChatGPT, Google (AI Overviews and AI Mode) and Perplexity. Add Gemini if your buyers work in Google's tools. Add Claude if your buyers are technical or use it for research. This is a judgment call, so check with your customers, and keep the same set each month.

Several vendors offer free checkers. Ahrefs has a free AI visibility checker that needs no signup. It queries ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Copilot and Google AI Overviews and shows total mentions, mentions by platform and the most cited domains and pages. Semrush has a free AI search visibility checker that you can use three times a day without an account. Both vendors sell paid tools, so treat these as a free preview.

A free checker is useful for a quick second opinion on your manual results. It is not a program. It tests a limited set of prompts that the tool chose, not the questions your own buyers ask. That is why the manual test above starts from your own sales and support questions. Prompt choice matters as much as the tool. If you pick 20 prompts that happen to favor your brand, you will look strong, and if you pick 20 that do not, you will look invisible.

What if your brand never appears?

First, check the branded prompts. If a tool cannot describe you even when asked by name, the problem is basic. Work through these in order:

  1. Access. Check that robots.txt, your firewall and your CDN allow the crawlers you want, and that your key pages are indexed in Google.
  2. Basic facts. Make sure your site clearly says who you are, what you sell, where you work and who runs the company, with the same facts on your profiles and listings.
  3. Third-party presence. Check whether you appear on the review sites, directories and lists that the tools cite for your category.
  4. Category pages. Check whether you have a page that answers the main buyer question directly.

Set expectations honestly. An Ahrefs author writes that, in their experience, established brands with strong SEO can see early wins in weeks to months, while new brands with little online authority may need 12 to 18 months. That is one practitioner's view, not a measured rule, but it shows why a new brand should not expect quick results.

Can you report one AI visibility score?

You can, but it hides the cause of any change. A single score blends mentions, links, accuracy and tools that behave differently. If it moves, you will not know why. For your team or your board, report the four numbers by prompt type and by tool, plus a short note on what changed. Add a single combined score only as a summary, and write down how it is calculated.

Branded search volume is a useful clue but it is not a replacement. It can rise when people hear about you in AI answers, and it can rise for many other reasons. Use it next to the prompt test, not instead of it.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Testing once. One run is a sample. The SparkToro study shows how much lists vary.
  • Chasing order. Whether you are first or third in a list changes from run to run. Focus on whether you appear.
  • Testing only branded prompts. You will look better than you are. Include category and comparison questions.
  • Changing the prompts each month. You cannot compare results if the questions differ.
  • Logging in while testing. Memory and history can change the answer.
  • Trusting one tool's score. Keep mentions, citations and accuracy separate.
  • Ignoring wrong answers. Inaccurate answers can cost deals, so track accuracy.
  • Buying mentions. Google says seeking fake mentions is not as helpful as it might seem.

A 90-day plan

Days 1 to 30: baseline

Run the 30-minute test. Log everything. Fix wrong facts and access problems that you find. Choose three to five competitors to track.

Days 31 to 60: fix and fill

Update your five key pages with direct answers and sources. Write one or two pages for the gaps. Correct third-party pages where you can. Repeat the test.

Days 61 to 90: earn and compare

Pitch one or two real stories to the sites the tools cite. Run the test a third time. Compare the rates with your baseline and decide what to repeat.
